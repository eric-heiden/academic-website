SHAC and SAPO locomotion with the MuJoCo Warp adjoint

Timestep, gradient horizon, action saturation, and gait quality when training Go1, H1, G1, and the MuJoCo Humanoid with the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp pull request #1535

Simulator
MuJoCo Warp PR #1535 at 357a75d
Software
Warp 1.15.0 · MuJoCo 3.12.1.dev · PyTorch 2.11.0
Hardware
One quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU (Multi-Instance GPU partition 1g.24gb) · 4 CPU cores

Abstract

Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) trains locomotion policies by back-propagating short simulated rollouts through a differentiable simulator; SAPO adds a normalized policy entropy to its rewards. This report trains both on the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp pull request #1535 at commit 357a75d. Implicit treatment of joint damping removes the explicit-damping instability (Go1 at 20 ms, MuJoCo Humanoid at 10 ms), and 10 ms steps produce 4.8–4.9 times more simulated time per second than 2 ms. Larger steps, however, made epochs of the first SHAC trainer only 2.3–2.7 times faster, shorten the horizon over which the policy gradient is informative, and change the dynamics enough that 20 ms Go1 policies fail in a 1 ms simulator. On Isaac Lab's Go1 velocity task, SHAC stood still because its tanh-squashed actions saturated: 97% of the action components, with a mean squash derivative of 0.02. SAPO's entropy terms, whose log-density penalizes saturation, prevented this, and every tested SAPO seed learned the full command set with a tracking error of 0.05 m/s; adding only a saturation penalty to SHAC also let it learn the task, about five times more slowly. With SHAC and without a gait prior, H1 walked only once the target critic followed the trained critic quickly; with that critic and added joint damping, the MuJoCo Humanoid ran. Without a gait prior, SAPO's G1 policies tracked commands but limped and crossed their feet; with a differentiable gait prior on foot heights and placement and with collisions between the feet, they walked with symmetric alternating steps (tracking error 0.09–0.10 m/s, three seeds). Per-foot gait measurements, not tracking error, revealed the difference.

1. Introduction

Can a policy for legged locomotion be trained from scratch by differentiating through MuJoCo Warp, and what does it take?

A differentiable simulator returns the derivative of the next state with respect to the current state and control. Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) uses these derivatives to train a policy by back-propagating short simulated rollouts, and a learned value function summarizes everything beyond the rollout [Xu et al. 2022]. Compared with reinforcement learning from sampled returns, this can need far fewer simulated steps, but only if the gradients stay informative through contact over the rollout. This report tests SHAC and its maximum-entropy variant SAPO [Xing et al. 2025] on the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp, the GPU implementation of MuJoCo, as proposed in pull request #1535 at commit 357a75d.

The study has two parts. The first asks whether the pull request's implicit time integration allows larger physics timesteps, and what larger timesteps do to throughput, gradient quality, and the learned policies, on the reference running tasks of the original SHAC work. The second trains velocity-tracking policies on tasks that follow Isaac Lab's locomotion environments, for the Unitree Go1 quadruped and the Unitree H1 and G1 humanoids, and returns to the classic MuJoCo Humanoid's running task. It identifies what prevented learning and what fixed it. Figures 1–4 show four of the resulting policies in a simulator with a finer timestep than the one they were trained in.

The main findings are as follows. Implicit damping removes the explicit-damping instability, but larger timesteps shorten the horizon over which the policy gradient is informative and change the dynamics enough that 20 ms policies fail at 1 ms (Sections 3 and 4). On the velocity-tracking tasks, SHAC's tanh-squashed actions saturate, which multiplies the analytic action gradient by about 0.02; SAPO's entropy terms contain a correction that penalizes saturation, and with them every tested seed learned the full Go1 command set; the correction alone also sufficed, but learned about five times more slowly (Section 4). With SHAC and without a gait prior, H1 needed a target critic that follows the trained critic quickly; the G1 walked cleanly only with SAPO and a differentiable gait prior on foot heights and placement, whereas without the prior it tracked the commands with a limp (Section 5).

2. Method

Both training algorithms differentiate short simulated rollouts through the MuJoCo Warp step adjoint. SAPO adds a normalized entropy to the rewards, and the tanh correction in that entropy turns out to matter for position-controlled robots.

Analytic step gradients in MuJoCo Warp

MuJoCo Warp (MJWarp) is a GPU implementation of MuJoCo. Pull request #1535 adds an analytic backward pass for one simulation step. MuJoCo computes accelerations by solving a convex constraint problem for contacts, joint limits, and friction. The pull request differentiates the converged solution with the implicit function theorem instead of differentiating the solver iterations. At commit 357a75d it supports the Newton constraint solver, elliptic and pyramidal friction cones, the Euler and implicitfast integrators, and eleven primitive collision pairs. Other pairs, such as capsule–cylinder, contribute no gradient. The velocity-tracking tasks therefore let robot geometry collide only with the floor, as Isaac Lab does for the robots used here; the final G1 configuration additionally lets its two feet collide with each other (Section 5).

The training harness captures each physics step and its adjoint as CUDA graphs and keeps the completed forward data of every physics step in a window, so that the backward pass applies the step adjoints in reverse order without recomputing the forward steps (Figure 5). The policy, written in PyTorch, and the simulator share one CUDA stream, and an epoch needs one host synchronization. The adjoint of the control input ignores actuator force limits. The harness therefore sets the control derivative of saturated actuators to zero.

Time integration

MuJoCo advances generalized positions \(q\) and velocities \(v\) with a physics timestep \(h\). The integrators considered here are semi-implicit: the new velocity updates the position, \(q_{k+1} = q_k \oplus h\,v_{k+1}\), where \(\oplus\) denotes integration on the configuration manifold. They differ in the acceleration used in \(v_{k+1} = v_k + h\,a_k\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{explicit:}&\quad M a_k = f_k,\\ \text{implicit damping:}&\quad (M + hB)\,a_k = f_k,\\ \texttt{implicitfast}\text{:}&\quad \big(M - h\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}\big)\,a_k = f_k. \end{aligned}\]

Here \(M\) is the joint-space inertia matrix, \(f_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)\) collects all applied, passive, bias, and constraint forces, \(u_k\) is the actuator control, and \(B\) is the diagonal matrix of joint damping coefficients. MuJoCo's Euler integrator uses the middle form by default. implicitfast also treats actuator velocity gains, such as the derivative gain of a position servo, implicitly, and omits Coriolis derivatives. For a joint with effective inertia \(I\) and damping \(b\), the explicit form multiplies the velocity by \(1 - hb/I\) per step and is unstable for \(h > h_\text{crit} = 2I/b\). Joints with small inertia and large damping therefore bound the timestep of explicit integration. The policy acts every \(\Delta t_c = 20\) ms and holds its control for \(K = \Delta t_c / h\) physics steps, so all objectives and metrics live on the same 20 ms control grid.

Short-Horizon Actor-Critic

SHAC [Xu et al. 2022] trains a stochastic policy \(\pi_\theta\) with parameters \(\theta\) on \(N\) parallel environments. Each epoch continues every environment for a window of \(T\) control steps from the states reached in the previous epoch and minimizes

\[L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t} r_t^{n} + \gamma^{T} V_{\bar\phi}\!\left(s_T^{n}\right)\right],\]

where \(r_t^n\) is the reward of environment \(n\) at step \(t\), \(\gamma = 0.99\) the discount factor, \(s_T^n\) the state at the end of the window, and \(V_{\bar\phi}\) a target critic. The gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\) is obtained by back-propagating through the policy and through every simulation step of the window. If an environment terminates inside the window, its sum stops there without a value term. The critic \(V_\phi\) regresses TD(\(\lambda\)) targets with \(\lambda = 0.95\), and the target critic follows it once per epoch with the Polyak update \(\bar\phi \leftarrow \alpha\,\bar\phi + (1 - \alpha)\,\phi\). The factor \(\alpha\) sets how quickly the bootstrapped value at the window end reflects the current critic: the original humanoid setting \(\alpha = 0.995\) gives the target a half-life of 138 epochs, \(\alpha = 0.9\) a half-life of 6.6 epochs. A longer window \(T\) exposes more consequences of an action to the gradient, but back-propagating through contact over many steps produces large and inconsistent gradients (Section 3).

The policy samples a pre-squash action \(u = \mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta \odot \epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and applies \(a = \tanh(u) \in (-1, 1)^d\), where \(d\) is the action dimension. For the position-controlled robots, \(a\) sets the targets \(q_\text{target} = \bar q + s_a\,a\) of proportional-derivative servos around a default pose \(\bar q\), with action scale \(s_a = 0.5\) rad. SHAC learns a state-independent standard deviation \(\sigma_\theta\), uses ELU networks with layer normalization, Adam, gradient-norm clipping at 1, and learning rates that decay linearly to \(10^{-5}\).

SAPO: entropy in the returns

Soft analytic policy optimization (SAPO) [Xing et al. 2025] keeps SHAC's window objective but replaces each reward by an entropy-augmented reward,

\[\begin{aligned} \tilde r_t &= r_t + \alpha_H\,\hat h_t,\\ \hat h_t &= \tfrac{1}{d}\big(-\log \pi_\theta + d/2\big),\\ \log \pi_\theta &= \textstyle\sum_j \big[\log \mathcal{N}_j(u_j) - \log(1 - a_j^2)\big]. \end{aligned}\]

Here \(\pi_\theta\) is evaluated at the sampled action \(a_t\) in state \(s_t\), \(\mathcal{N}_j\) is the Gaussian density with mean \(\mu_j\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_j\), and the second term accounts for the squashing. The one-sample entropy estimate \(-\log \pi_\theta\) is shifted by the target entropy \(\bar{\mathcal{H}} = -d/2\) and scaled by \(d\), so that \(\hat h_t\) lies roughly in \([0, 1]\) and is comparable across robots. The temperature \(\alpha_H\) starts at 1 and is adjusted by gradient steps on \(-\log \alpha_H \,\big(\mathbb{E}[\log \pi_\theta] + \bar{\mathcal{H}}\big)\), which lowers it when the policy's entropy exceeds the target. The augmented reward \(\tilde r_t\) enters both the actor objective \(L(\theta)\) and the critic's TD targets. SAPO further uses a state-dependent standard deviation \(\sigma_\theta(s)\) with \(\log \sigma \in [-5, 2]\), two critics whose mean bootstraps the actor and whose minimum enters the targets, no target network, SiLU activations, AdamW, gradient clipping at 0.5, and learning rates of \(2 \cdot 10^{-3}\) (actor) and \(5 \cdot 10^{-4}\) (critic). All other settings are those of SHAC.

Saturated actions and the analytic gradient

Every analytic gradient that reaches the policy passes through the squashing function:

\[\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_j} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j}\,\big(1 - a_j^2\big).\]

When an action component saturates, \(|a_j| \to 1\), the factor \(1 - a_j^2\) goes to zero and the simulator gradient no longer moves that component, however informative it is. The squash term of the log-density does the opposite in SAPO: since \(-\log \pi_\theta\) contains \(+\sum_j \log(1 - a_j^2)\), which tends to \(-\infty\) at saturation, the entropy reward penalizes saturated actions and its gradient pushes them back. To test this mechanism in isolation, a squash penalty adds only this term to SHAC's reward,

\[r_t \leftarrow r_t + \frac{w_\text{sq}}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\big(1 - a_{t,j}^2\big),\]

with a fixed weight \(w_\text{sq} = 0.3\). Unlike the entropy reward, it does not reward a larger standard deviation.

Gait prior

The analytic gradient treats the contact set as fixed within a step, so it cannot value a foot that is about to lift off or touch down. A gait clock with period \(P = 0.8\) s gives foot \(i\) at control step \(t\) the phase \(\psi_i = (t\,\Delta t_c/P + \delta_i) \bmod 1\), with offsets \(\delta = (0, 0.5)\) for the left and right foot. A foot is in stance while \(\psi_i < D\), with duty factor \(D = 0.6\), and otherwise in swing with progress \(\rho_i = (\psi_i - D)/(1 - D) \in [0, 1)\). With \(p_i\) a point under the sole of foot \(i\), computed by differentiable forward kinematics, three terms are added to the reward:

\[\begin{aligned} r_\text{gait} = &-w_h \textstyle\sum_i \big(p_{i,z} - z_i^\text{ref}\big)^2\\ &- w_s \textstyle\sum_{i:\,\psi_i < D} \lVert \dot p_{i,xy} \rVert^2\\ &- w_r \textstyle\sum_{i:\,\rho_i \ge 0.5} \lVert e_i \rVert^2,\\ z_i^\text{ref} = &\; z_0 + z_\text{sw} \sin(\pi \rho_i)\,[\psi_i \ge D],\\ e_i = &\; \tilde p_{i,xy} - \bar p_{i,xy} - \kappa_v v_b - \kappa_e (v_b - c_{xy}). \end{aligned}\]

The first term asks the foot to follow a swing arc of height \(z_\text{sw} = 0.08\) m above its standing height \(z_0\); \([\cdot]\) is 1 when its condition holds. The second penalizes the horizontal speed \(\dot p_{i,xy}\) of feet in clock stance, computed from the displacement over one control step. The third is a Raibert-type placement rule for the second half of the swing, with placement error \(e_i\): \(\tilde p_{i,xy}\) is the foot position relative to the base in the heading frame, \(\bar p_{i,xy}\) its position in the default pose, \(v_b\) the planar base velocity in that frame, and \(\kappa_v = \kappa_e = 0.1\) s. Placing the foot ahead in proportion to the velocity arrests a fall; the term in \(v_b - c_{xy}\) shifts the step to accelerate or brake. The policy observes \(\sin(2\pi t\Delta t_c/P)\) and \(\cos(2\pi t\Delta t_c/P)\). H1 uses \(w_h = 40\), \(w_s = 2\), \(w_r = 10\); the G1 uses \(w_h = 120\) and \(w_r = 20\) (units m⁻² for \(w_h\) and \(w_r\), s²/m² for \(w_s\)). In the SAPO configurations of the G1, commands with planar speed and yaw rate below 0.1 hold both feet in clock stance, so the prior asks the robot to stand rather than step in place.

Evaluation

Policies are evaluated deterministically (\(a = \tanh(\mu_\theta)\)) in a reference simulator, implicitfast with \(h = 1\) ms and the robot's original contact parameters, from 256 randomized starts for 10 s, with commands drawn as in training. The tracking error is the mean norm of the difference between the commanded and the measured planar base velocity; a policy walks when this error is below 0.3 m/s, since standing still gives 0.5–1.0 m/s. Gait quality is measured per foot under fixed commands from the simulator's foot–floor contacts and the foot positions: the duration and clearance of airborne phases, the stride between touchdowns, and the share of a foot's horizontal travel that happens while it is in contact, called the drag share. The alternation is the share of consecutive touchdowns made by different feet.

3. Timestep, throughput, and gradient horizon

Implicit damping removes the explicit-damping stability limit and larger timesteps make the simulator faster, but they shorten the horizon over which the policy gradient stays informative.

Stability and accuracy

For five robots (Ant, Go1, Go1 with its damping moved into the servos as an actuator velocity gain, the MuJoCo Humanoid, and H1 with its original torque motors) and each timestep \(h \in \{1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20\}\) ms, 1024 worlds received the same temporally correlated random actions, \(u_t = \operatorname{clip}(0.9\,u_{t-1} + 0.45\,\xi_t, -1, 1)\) with standard normal \(\xi_t\), for 10 s. A world diverged when its state became non-finite, a velocity exceeded \(10^3\), or the root left the height band from −1 to 20 m. The explicit-damping bound \(h_\text{crit}\) predicts every damping-driven divergence (Table 2): the Go1 (\(h_\text{crit} = 14.7\) ms) is stable at 10 ms and diverges at 20 ms, and the Humanoid (\(h_\text{crit} = 10.1\) ms) diverges at 10 ms. Implicit damping removes these divergences. It does not make 20 ms safe in general: 1.9% of Humanoid worlds and about 70% of H1 worlds diverge at 20 ms with either implicit form, from stiff dynamics other than damping.

Euler with implicit damping and implicitfast give identical trajectories for these robots, because their only velocity-dependent smooth force is joint damping. When the Go1's damping is moved into its servos, only implicitfast treats it implicitly and halves the joint error. Accuracy degrades in proportion to the timestep (Figure 6): the median joint error after one 20 ms control step, measured against a 0.25 ms reference from 4096 sampled states, grows for the Go1 from 0.066° at 1 ms to 0.90° at 10 ms and 2.1° at 20 ms.

Throughput

These measurements use the first version of the harness, whose backward pass recomputes each step before applying its adjoint (Section 6). Replaying captured CUDA graphs is 2.7–4.7 times faster than launching each kernel from Python for a forward and backward step. A recomputed step with its adjoint costs 5–6 forward steps, and implicitfast costs 11–17% more than Euler, mostly in the backward pass. Between 2 and 10 ms the cost of one physics step barely changes, so 10 ms produces 4.8–4.9 times more simulated time per second than 2 ms. A SHAC epoch, however, also pays for the policy, the rewards, and the critic update, which do not shrink with the timestep: with 256 environments an epoch at 10 ms is only 2.3 (Ant) and 2.4 (Go1) times faster than at 2 ms, and 2.6–2.7 times with 1024 environments.

Gradient horizon

The step adjoint agrees with central finite differences where the dynamics are smooth: over five control steps it is within 5% of the finite difference in 73% (Humanoid) to 89% (Go1) of worlds, with median relative errors of 0.05–0.3%. In the other worlds the finite difference does not converge as the perturbation shrinks, which marks a contact activating, a stick–slip transition, or a joint limit engaging inside the perturbation. What SHAC needs is the gradient of a multi-step return. For a fixed policy and 256 start states, the return over \(T\) control steps is

\[J_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t^n\,\chi_t^n ,\]

with \(\chi_t^n \in \{0, 1\}\) equal to 1 until environment \(n\) terminates. The half-batch cosine compares \(\nabla_\theta J_T\) computed on two disjoint halves of the batch. It is near 1 when the gradient direction is a property of the policy and the task, and near 0 when a few chaotic or discontinuous rollouts dominate. Figure 7 shows that the timestep dominates beyond 8 control steps. For the Go1 at 2 ms the gradient stays consistent (cosine ≥ 0.97) to 32 steps, with the analytic slope equal to the finite-difference slope; at 20 ms the cosine is 0.41 at 8 steps and near zero at 16. On the fall-dominated states visited by a learning MuJoCo Humanoid, the gradient is consistent for only about four steps. Softer contacts and a fourfold solver budget did not restore consistency at 10 ms. A plausible explanation, not confirmed here, is that contact sets and stick–slip states are fixed per physics step, so a larger step changes the discrete dynamics by a larger jump that the local gradient misses. Most training therefore uses windows of 8 or 16 control steps.

4. Quadrupeds

SHAC learns the reference running tasks at every timestep, but only small-step policies transfer. On Isaac Lab's Go1 velocity task it stands still because its squashed actions saturate; SAPO's entropy terms prevent this.

Reference tasks and the timestep

Ant and Go1 were trained with SHAC on the reference tasks with explicit Euler at 2 ms and with implicitfast at 2, 5, 10, and 20 ms, three seeds each, 1000 epochs of 256 environments and an 8-step window. Only the simulator differs between configurations. Per epoch, the 2 ms configurations learned best, and explicit and implicit integration at 2 ms gave similar results (Figure 8, Table 3). The Ant's speed in the reference simulator fell from 6.3–6.4 m/s at 2 ms to 2.7 m/s at 10 ms, in the order of gradient quality. The Go1's reward saturates at 1.5 m/s, and its 2, 5, and 10 ms policies reached that speed and kept it in the reference simulator.

The 20 ms Go1 policies did not transfer: 1.55 m/s in their training simulator, 0.84 m/s with 2.6 m of lateral drift in the reference simulator, where the seed-0 policy settles into a crouch (Figure 11). A larger timestep also does not buy accuracy with more epochs: the Ant trained for 4000 epochs at 10 ms, 1.8 times the wall-clock time of a 2 ms run, reached 5.6 m/s in its training simulator but only 2.7 m/s in the reference simulator. Go1 policies trained at 10 ms or less behaved almost identically at every evaluation timestep up to 10 ms (Table A2), so below this limit the simulated dynamics are effectively converged; above it, training optimizes a different physical system.

Velocity tracking: the standing optimum

On Isaac Lab's Go1 task, which commands forward, lateral, and yaw velocities from \([-1, 1]\) (m/s or rad/s) and the Isaac Lab equivalent Go1 of Table 1, SHAC learned to stand: 95% survival and a tracking error of 0.77 m/s, close to the 0.75 m/s expected magnitude of the commanded velocity. Restricting the commands, changing the robot model, the tracking kernel, the action range, or the window, adding a trot-pattern gait prior, or a command curriculum did not help (Table A3). Proximal policy optimization (PPO), a reinforcement-learning method that uses no simulator gradients, learned the task with Isaac Lab's settings, including unbounded Gaussian actions scaled by 0.25 rad; with SHAC's scale of 0.5 rad, PPO also stood still.

Two problems were found. The first is in the robot model. The analytic adjoint occasionally gave a single world a gradient 10 to more than 100 times the median over worlds, which can dominate a batch update. The spikes come from the impedance of the Menagerie Go1 feet, which rises to its maximum \(d_\text{max} = 1\) at 23 mm of penetration. MuJoCo regularizes a contact in proportion to \((1 - d)/d\) and clamps \(d\) at 0.9999, so the constraint becomes nearly rigid. With \(d_\text{max} = 0.95\) in training (evaluation keeps the original feet), the spikes disappeared (Table A4), and SHAC learned the forward-command variant with all four seeds, against none without the change. It still stood on the full command set, with 512 and with 2048 environments (Table 4).

The second problem is in the policy parameterization. Figure 12 follows the training tracking error and the saturation of the sampled actions. Within 50 epochs, SHAC drove 52% of the action components to \(|a| > 0.95\), and 97% by the end. The mean squash derivative \(1 - a^2\), which multiplies every analytic action gradient, fell to 0.02: the joint targets sit at the ends of their ±0.5 rad range, where the gradient that could move the policy away from standing nearly vanishes. SAPO kept saturation at 2% or less and the derivative between 0.72 and 0.80, and learned the full task with both seeds (tracking error 0.05 m/s).

Table 4 separates the causes. Removing SAPO's entropy terms brought back the standing optimum, and SAPO's optimizer settings alone (activations, AdamW, clipping, learning rates, no target network) did not escape it. The state-dependent standard deviation and the second critic were not needed. Adding only the entropy terms to SHAC, or only the squash penalty, made SHAC learn the task with both seeds. The squash penalty does not reward a larger standard deviation, and with it the standard deviation fell from 0.37 to 0.11 during training, below SHAC's 0.30. Saturation therefore explains the standing optimum. The noise scale may explain the speed: with the squash penalty alone, the training tracking error fell below 0.3 m/s only after 1090 and 1217 epochs, against 200 and 215 epochs with the entropy terms, whose Gaussian part raised the standard deviation to about 0.4 early in training, and 181 with SAPO (Figure 12). Removing the tanh did not help: the unbounded actions grew to a mean magnitude of 9–29, so the joint targets lay beyond the joint ranges, where MuJoCo clamps the control and its gradient is zero.

5. Humanoids

Balance requires stepping, which the local gradient cannot value. A fast target critic, joint damping, a gait prior, and SAPO each address part of this; the G1 needs the prior for a clean gait.

Why the reference tasks fail

On the SHAC reference task, no tested configuration taught the MuJoCo Humanoid to balance. Seventeen variants, with timesteps of 2, 5, and 10 ms, windows of 8 to 32 steps, stronger actuators, a standing task, softer contacts, lower learning rates, clipped adjoints, and full-episode back-propagation, ended with mean episodes of 0.77–1.21 s, barely longer than the 0.66–0.82 s of the idle robot (Table A1). With the velocity term, the policies converged to a forward dive (Figure 14). The critic was informative, and the step adjoint was accurate, but on the falling states these policies visit, the policy gradient stays consistent for only about four control steps (Section 3). A humanoid also cannot stand by stiffening: holding H1 upright as an inverted pendulum about the ankle would need an ankle stiffness near \(mgl \approx 500\) N·m/rad, where \(m\) is the mass, \(g\) gravitational acceleration, and \(l\) the height of the center of mass, and H1's ankle provides at most 40 N·m. Balance therefore requires stepping, which changes the set of active contacts. The adjoint differentiates the solution for the current contact set and cannot represent the benefit of a foot contact that does not exist yet.

What made the humanoids learn

The Isaac Lab tasks of Section 2 changed four things for H1 and G1 together: torso–floor contact instead of a height threshold as termination, floor-only collisions (the Menagerie H1 has 127 self-collision pairs without adjoint support), initial states without penetration (half of the reference task's Humanoid resets started up to 14 cm inside the floor), and Isaac Lab's servo gains. With SHAC's original target critic (\(\alpha = 0.995\)), H1 then walked with the gait prior, and not without it (one seed, 600 epochs). With \(\alpha = 0.9\), the bootstrapped value at the window end follows the critic within a few epochs instead of about a hundred, and H1 learned 1.7–2.3 times as fast per epoch with the prior and also walked without it (Table 6, Figure 15). The MuJoCo Humanoid ran only when two changes were combined (Figure 4, Table A8): \(\alpha = 0.9\) and a joint damping of 10 N·m·s/rad, which the original SHAC simulator applies to every joint. Damping raised the half-batch cosine of a random policy's gradient at 32 control steps from 0.09 to 0.82 (Table A9).

SAPO walked on H1 without the prior as accurately as SHAC (0.10 ± 0.04 against 0.12 ± 0.02 m/s). On the G1, SHAC balanced but did not walk, both without the prior and with H1's prior weights; with stronger prior weights (\(w_h = 120\), \(w_r = 20\)) and without lateral commands it walked with two of three seeds. SAPO without the prior tracked the full command set with errors of 0.21 / 0.14 m/s. Tracking error, however, does not show how the robot walks.

Under fixed commands, the per-foot measurements of Section 2 expose the difference (Table 7, Figure 16). Without collisions between the legs, they also crossed their feet: the left foot was not to the left of the right foot in up to 38–83% of the steps of a command. The SHAC policy with the prior alternated its feet but lifted one foot lower and shorter than the other, and fell under lateral commands, on which it was not trained.

6. Training cost

Keeping each physics step's forward data removes the recomputation in the backward pass. With the 0.16 s window, SAPO adds about 10% to a SHAC epoch.

The first version of the harness recomputed every physics step during the backward pass before applying its adjoint. The step adjoint needs only the step's input and its completed forward data, so the final trainer keeps one simulator state buffer per physics step of the window, each with its own captured forward and backward graph. On identical inputs, the action gradients of a 4-step window then differ from the recomputing backward by a relative norm of \(1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}\) for the Go1 and \(4.9 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for H1; two recomputing runs differ by as much, because the GPU's contact detection is not deterministic. The backward pass no longer runs the constraint solver, which saves the most when many physics steps make up one control step (Table 8). Together with a captured critic update, resets without host synchronization, and batched forward kinematics, the final trainer runs an epoch of the reference-task Ant and Go1 configurations 1.75 times faster at 2 ms.

7. Limitations

  • Seeds. The reference-task results use three seeds per configuration, and the velocity-tracking comparisons one to four; several screening rows in the appendix use one seed. Shares such as “two of two seeds” separate configurations that always or never walk, but they are not reliable success rates for configurations in between, such as SHAC with the mjlab reward (one of two) or per-world clipping on the full Go1 task (one of four).
  • Mechanism. The saturation measurements of Figure 12 use one seed per configuration. The squash penalty was tested with one weight (0.3). That saturation is the cause of the standing optimum is supported by the ablation, but other effects of the entropy terms, for example on the critic targets, were not separated.
  • Stability and accuracy protocol. Divergence was measured under random actions from near-standing states, and accuracy over one control step. Trained policies visit fewer states, and long-horizon differences between timesteps are dominated by chaotic divergence.
  • Task fidelity. The tasks follow Isaac Lab's definitions but omit observation noise, mass randomization, pushes, and the heading controller. The Go1 servo model approximates Isaac Lab's learned actuator network. The G1 policy controls only the legs; its waist and arms hold their default pose. The Go2 environment that motivated the mjlab reward and the 0.54 s window was not reproduced: it uses a different robot, servo gains, control rate (60 Hz), and Newton's Featherstone solver.
  • Robot models. The running MuJoCo Humanoid uses a joint damping of 10 N·m·s/rad, as in the original SHAC work but not in MuJoCo's model. The Go1 was trained with a foot impedance maximum of 0.95 instead of the Menagerie value of 1 and evaluated with the original feet. On the velocity-tracking tasks, except for the final G1's feet, robot geometry collides only with the floor, so legs can pass through each other; the H1 policy's feet almost never overlapped (in less than 0.1% of steps), and the Go1's legs were not checked. The gait prior fixes the step period (0.8 s) and duty factor, so the G1 cannot adapt its cadence to the commanded speed. With colliding feet, the G1 policies survived 98.4–99.6% of rollouts with random commands, and one of three seeds taps a foot when commanded to stand; three seeds do not establish a failure rate.
  • Adjoint of the tested commit. The control derivative ignores actuator force limits (corrected in the harness); mesh collisions, tendons, and equality constraints are unsupported; collision pairs outside the eleven supported primitive pairs contribute no gradient without a warning; and the commit has no adjoint tests. For Euler with implicit joint damping, the velocity derivative with respect to joint positions omits the configuration dependence of the mass matrix (56% and 430% relative error on a damped two-link pendulum); the training runs used implicitfast or explicit Euler, which are not affected.
  • Hardware and timing. All timings come from one MIG 1g.24gb partition, a quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, with four CPU cores. Processes outside this study were not controlled; two timing runs gave bimodal epoch times (about 90 or 190 ms for the same configuration) and were discarded.

8. Conclusion

The MuJoCo Warp step adjoint at commit 357a75d is accurate where the dynamics are smooth, and its implicit integration removes the damping instability that limited explicit integration to about 10 ms for the Go1 and 5 ms for the Humanoid. Larger timesteps nevertheless pay off less than the simulator speedup suggests. A SHAC epoch of the first trainer at 10 ms is only 2.3–2.7 times faster than at 2 ms, the policy gradient stays informative over fewer control steps, and policies trained at 20 ms exploit dynamics that a 1 ms simulator does not reproduce. In this study, 5 ms with windows of 8–16 control steps was a safe operating point.

On velocity tracking, the main obstacle was not the simulator gradient but the policy parameterization. SHAC's tanh-squashed actions saturated within a few dozen epochs on the Isaac Lab Go1 task, and the factor \(1 - a^2\) then suppressed the analytic gradient by about fifty times; the policy stood still. SAPO's entropy terms contain the tanh correction of the log-density, which penalizes saturation. With SAPO, every tested seed learned the full Go1 command set, with either reward. The ablation and the squash penalty indicate that this correction removes the standing optimum: SAPO without its entropy terms stood still, and SHAC with only the squash penalty learned the task. With the full entropy terms, which also keep the exploration noise larger early in training, learning was about five times faster. SHAC implementations with bounded actions may need such terms. A rigid foot contact in the Menagerie Go1 model, with impedance maximum 1, separately caused rare per-world gradient spikes.

The humanoids needed more. With SHAC, H1 walked without a gait prior only once the target critic followed the trained critic quickly (Polyak factor 0.9 instead of 0.995); SAPO, which has no target network, also walked without the prior. Joint damping as in the original SHAC simulator, together with the faster target critic, made the MuJoCo Humanoid run. The G1 tracked commands without a prior but limped, dragging one foot and crossing its feet. A differentiable gait prior on foot heights and placement, which tells the gradient when each foot should be in the air, gave it a symmetric alternating gait on the full command set, and letting its feet collide reduced foot interpenetration to at most 0.3% of the steps at a small cost in survival. Tracking accuracy alone did not reveal these gait defects; per-foot gait measurements did.

Bundled contact gradients (Tables A10 and A11)

Bundled contact gradients [Aditya et al. 2026] continue a rollout from the average of several perturbed copies of an environment when a contact force exceeds a threshold, which keeps the forward and backward passes consistent. The implementation here simulates \(N\) environments with \(B\) branches as \(NB\) worlds, perturbs the policy’s joint positions and velocities in antithetic pairs, and averages after every control step. The averaging does not remove single-world spikes, which are only divided by \(B\), and more branches give more chances for one (Table A10). At equal world count, bundled Go1 training did not walk with the original foot contact (Table A11); on the H1 task with the gait prior, 64 environments with 8 branches did not reach the threshold of Table A6 within 600 epochs (reference simulator: 73% survival, 0.57 m/s).

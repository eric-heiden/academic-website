SHAC locomotion in MuJoCo Warp: implicit integration and retuned tasks

Timestep, throughput, gradient quality, training outcomes, and a retuning study with the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp pull request #1535

Simulator

Software

Abstract

Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) trains control policies by back-propagating short simulated rollouts through a differentiable simulator. This report evaluates SHAC on the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp pull request #1535, whose current revision supports implicit time integration. It asks whether implicit integration allows larger physics timesteps, whether larger timesteps raise useful training throughput, and whether quadruped and humanoid locomotion can be learned from scratch. Treating joint damping implicitly removes the explicit-damping instability that limited earlier configurations (Go1 diverges at 20 ms and the MuJoCo Humanoid at 10 ms with explicit damping). For the tested robots, MuJoCo's default Euler integrator already provides this benefit, and implicitfast adds accuracy only for damping inside the actuators. Going from 2 to 10 ms raises simulator throughput with gradients by 4.8–4.9 times but shortens complete SHAC epochs by only 2.3–2.7 times. Larger timesteps, however, shorten the horizon over which the analytic policy gradient stays informative, from at least 32 control steps at 2 ms to about 4 at 20 ms for Go1. They also change the simulated dynamics enough that policies trained at 10–20 ms can fail to transfer to a fine-step simulator. With the reference tasks, SHAC learned Ant and Go1 locomotion from scratch in every tested simulator configuration. The best Ant policies came from 2 ms training, and Go1 policies trained at up to 10 ms transferred to a fine-step simulator without loss. With these tasks it did not learn balance for the MuJoCo Humanoid in any of seventeen configurations, nor for a position-controlled Unitree H1 with a joint-angle gait clock; the diagnostics point to the inability of local gradients to value contact changes such as a new foot placement.

A second part modifies the tasks to follow Isaac Lab's locomotion environments and reduces the trainer's cost. Keeping each physics step's forward data in memory removes the recomputation in the backward pass and shortens training epochs by 1.3–2.6 times. With Isaac Lab's H1 task definition and a differentiable gait prior on foot heights and foot placement, SHAC learned Unitree H1 walking policies: over three seeds they survive 99.5% of 10 s rollouts in a 1 ms reference simulator and track velocity commands with a mean error of 0.15 ± 0.02 m/s, after 1500 epochs (an estimated 9.3 minutes of training). Without the foot-placement term the prior did not produce walking. For the Unitree G1, the prior combined with Isaac Lab's servo gains and a faster-updating target critic produced balance (89% of rollouts survive 10 s) but not walking. On an Isaac-Lab-style Go1 task, SHAC and proximal policy optimization (PPO) with SHAC's bounded action range converged to standing still, while PPO with Isaac Lab's unbounded actions learned the task; with the original Menagerie Go1, the outcome of SHAC depended on the random seed.

1. Background and question

Analytic simulator gradients can train policies directly, but only if the gradients stay informative over the planning horizon and the simulator is fast enough to generate millions of transitions.

Short-horizon actor-critic

Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) [Xu et al. 2022] trains a policy \(\pi_\theta\) by differentiating simulated returns with respect to the policy parameters \(\theta\). Each training epoch rolls out \(N\) parallel environments for \(T\) control steps from the states reached in the previous epoch and minimizes

\[L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t} r_t^{n} + \gamma^{T} V_\phi\!\left(s_T^{n}\right)\right],\]

where \(r_t^n\) is the reward of environment \(n\) at control step \(t\), \(\gamma = 0.99\) is the discount factor, \(s_T^n\) is the state at the end of the window, and \(V_\phi\) is a learned value function (the critic) with parameters \(\phi\). The gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\) is obtained by back-propagating through the policy and through every simulation step in the window. The critic summarizes everything beyond the window; it is trained separately on temporal-difference targets with \(\lambda = 0.95\). If an environment terminates inside the window (for example because the robot falls), its sum stops there and the value term is zero. A longer window exposes more of the consequences of an action to the gradient, but back-propagating through contact-rich dynamics over many steps can produce gradients that are large and inconsistent, and this effect is the main practical limit on \(T\).

The MuJoCo Warp adjoint

MuJoCo Warp (MJWarp) is a GPU implementation of MuJoCo. Pull request #1535 adds an analytic backward pass for one simulation step. MuJoCo computes accelerations by solving a convex constraint problem for contacts, joint limits, and friction; the pull request differentiates the converged solution with the implicit function theorem instead of differentiating the solver iterations. At the commit tested here, the backward pass supports the Newton constraint solver, elliptic and pyramidal friction cones, eleven primitive collision pairs (plane with sphere, capsule, ellipsoid, cylinder, or box; sphere with sphere, box, capsule, or cylinder; capsule–capsule; and capsule–box; other pairs contribute no gradient), and two integrators: Euler and implicitfast. An earlier SHAC study on this pull request, archived here, used an older commit, explicit time integration, and hybrid policies that were first trained with other methods.

Time integration

MuJoCo advances generalized positions \(q\) and velocities \(v\) with a timestep \(h\). All integrators considered here are semi-implicit: the new velocity is used to update the position, \(q_{k+1} = q_k \oplus h\,v_{k+1}\), where \(\oplus\) denotes integration on the configuration manifold (quaternions for free joints). They differ in how the acceleration used for \(v_{k+1} = v_k + h\,a_k\) is computed:

\[\underbrace{M a_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)}_{\text{explicit damping}},\qquad \underbrace{(M + hB)\,a_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)}_{\text{implicit joint damping}},\qquad \underbrace{\left(M - h\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}\right) a_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)}_{\texttt{implicitfast}}.\]

Here \(M\) is the joint-space inertia matrix, \(f\) collects all applied, passive, bias, and constraint forces, \(u_k\) is the actuator control, and \(B\) is the diagonal matrix of joint damping coefficients. MuJoCo's Euler integrator uses the middle form by default; disabling its eulerdamp option gives the explicit form on the left, which is the configuration of the earlier study. implicitfast additionally treats all velocity-dependent smooth forces implicitly, including actuator velocity gains such as the derivative gain of a position servo, while omitting Coriolis and centrifugal derivatives. A robot whose only velocity-dependent force is joint damping is therefore integrated identically by the last two forms.

For a single joint with effective inertia \(I\) and damping coefficient \(b\), the explicit form multiplies the velocity by \(1 - hb/I\) in each step when no other force acts. The update is unstable when

\[h > h_{\text{crit}} = \frac{2 I}{b}.\]

Joints with small inertia and large damping, such as the lower legs of a quadruped or the limbs of a humanoid, therefore bound the usable timestep of explicit integration. In this report \(I\) is the effective inertia \(1/(M^{-1})_{ii}\) of joint \(i\) at the nominal pose.

Question

The study asks whether implicit integration in the current pull request allows larger simulation timesteps, whether larger timesteps increase useful training throughput, and whether SHAC trained on the analytic adjoint learns locomotion for quadrupeds and humanoids from scratch. A second part (Section 8) modifies the tasks to follow the locomotion environments of Isaac Lab, NVIDIA's robot-learning framework, reduces the cost of the trainer, and asks which changes make humanoid locomotion learnable and training cheaper.

Control and physics timesteps

The policy acts every \(\Delta t_c = 20\) ms and holds its control constant over \(K = \Delta t_c / h\) physics steps. The physics timestep \(h\) ranges from 1 to 20 ms, so \(K\) ranges from 20 to 1. The training objective and all evaluation metrics are defined on the 20 ms control grid, which makes configurations with different \(h\) directly comparable.

Training harness

The harness follows the checkpointing scheme of the pull request's own differentiable-simulation examples. One physics step and its analytic backward pass are each captured once as a CUDA graph. During a rollout, the forward graph is replayed \(K\) times per control step while the step inputs (positions, velocities, and the solver's warm start) are saved. During back-propagation, the saved inputs are restored in reverse order and the backward graph is replayed, which recomputes the step and applies the analytic adjoint. PyTorch and Warp share one CUDA stream, so no host synchronization is needed between the policy and the simulator (Figure 1).

Two corrections were needed on top of the pull request. First, the adjoint of the control input ignores actuator force limits: it returns a nonzero derivative even when a servo is saturated and its force no longer depends on the control. The harness zeroes the control derivative of saturated actuators, using the actuator force recomputed during the backward replay. Second, the upstream adjoint tests were removed in the refactor that produced this commit, so gradient correctness was checked independently against finite differences (Section 5).

SHAC configuration

The SHAC implementation follows the reference code: a tanh-squashed Gaussian policy with a learned, state-independent log standard deviation initialized to −1; multilayer perceptrons with ELU activations and layer normalization; observation normalization frozen during each rollout; a TD(\(\lambda\)) critic with 16 passes over 4 minibatches per epoch and a Polyak-averaged target network; Adam with \(\beta = (0.7, 0.95)\); learning rates decayed linearly to \(10^{-5}\); and gradient-norm clipping at 1. Training uses 256 environments and 1000 epochs. The window length \(T\) is 8 control steps (0.16 s) unless stated otherwise; Section 5 explains this choice.

Observations contain the torso height, orientation, and velocities, the joint positions and scaled velocities, the uprightness and heading projections, and the previous action. Rewards follow the reference Ant and Humanoid tasks: forward velocity, plus \(0.1\) times uprightness, plus heading alignment, plus a height term, minus an action penalty for humanoids. Episodes last 1000 control steps (20 s) and end early when the torso falls below a height threshold. The reference reward caused the Go1 to gallop at about 4 m/s with extreme hip flexion, so the Go1 velocity term is capped at a target of 1.5 m/s, and lateral velocity and action changes are penalized. Resets perturb the root position by up to ±0.1 m, the orientation by up to 7.5°, the joint angles by up to ±0.2 rad, and all velocities by up to ±0.25.

Evaluation

Each trained policy is evaluated deterministically (tanh of the Gaussian mean) for 20 s from 256 randomized starts, both in its training simulator and in a common reference simulator: implicitfast with \(h = 1\) ms, which is finer than every training configuration. A policy that depends on artifacts of a large timestep loses performance in the reference simulator. Reported metrics are the survival fraction (episodes without a fall), the forward speed (forward displacement divided by the time survived), and the lateral drift.

3. Integrator stability and accuracy

Implicit damping removes the explicit-damping stability limit, but it does not remove other limits, and the one-step error grows linearly with the timestep for every integrator.

Protocol

For each model, integrator, and timestep \(h \in \{1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20\}\) ms, 1024 worlds start from the nominal pose with small random joint and velocity perturbations and receive the same temporally correlated random actions for 10 s. Actions follow \(a_t = \operatorname{clip}(0.9\,a_{t-1} + 0.45\,\epsilon_t, -1, 1)\) with standard normal \(\epsilon_t\), a vigorous excitation that makes most robots fall and flail. A world is counted as diverged when its state becomes non-finite, a generalized velocity exceeds \(10^3\), or the root leaves the height band from −1 to 20 m.

Accuracy is measured locally. States are sampled at four time points from a reference rollout (implicitfast, \(h = 0.25\) ms), giving 4096 samples per model. Each sample is advanced by one 20 ms control interval with the sampled action, in the reference and in every tested configuration. The reported error is the median over samples of the root-mean-square joint-angle difference after that interval. Repeating the reference step with \(h = 0.125\) ms changes the result by at most 0.05° and 0.03 mm, far below the differences reported here.

4. Throughput

Graph replay and a larger timestep each make the simulator several times faster. Larger timesteps shorten SHAC epochs by only a factor of two to three, because the learner and per-control-step costs do not shrink.

All timings in this section were taken with the GPU otherwise idle. Simulator throughput is reported as world-steps per second, the number of parallel worlds times the number of physics steps completed per second. A training configuration needs \(K = 20\text{ ms}/h\) physics steps per control step, so for a fixed cost per physics step, simulated time per wall-clock second grows in proportion to \(h\).

5. Gradient quality

The analytic gradients agree with finite differences where the dynamics are smooth. How far they stay informative along a rollout depends strongly on the physics timestep: larger steps shorten the usable horizon.

Correctness of the step adjoint

For 64 worlds per model, the directional derivative of a five-control-step objective with respect to the control sequence was compared with central finite differences, world by world. With perturbation size \(\varepsilon = 0.01\), the analytic value is within 5% of the finite difference in 73% (Humanoid) to 89% (Go1) of worlds, with median relative errors of 0.05–0.3%. In the remaining worlds the finite difference does not converge as \(\varepsilon\) decreases, which identifies a discontinuity inside the perturbation interval, such as a contact activating, a stick–slip transition, or a joint limit engaging. A contact-free, zero-gravity Ant without joint limits matches finite differences to three or four digits over 4 control steps and matches the smallest-perturbation finite difference over 16 steps; enabling joint limits introduces a gap, because the analytic gradient describes only the local dynamics and does not include the jump caused by a limit activating.

Policy gradients along a rollout

SHAC needs the gradient of a multi-step return with respect to the policy parameters. For a fixed policy and a fixed batch of 256 start states, define

\[J_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t^n\,\alpha_t^n,\]

where \(\alpha_t^n \in \{0, 1\}\) stops the sum after environment \(n\) terminates. Two diagnostics are reported. The half-batch cosine is the cosine similarity between \(\nabla_\theta J_T\) computed on two disjoint halves of the batch. It is near 1 when the gradient direction is a property of the policy and the task, and near 0 when a few chaotic or discontinuous rollouts dominate. The slope ratio divides the analytic directional derivative along the normalized gradient by the finite-difference derivative, which is the median over three perturbation sizes. It is near 1 when the analytic gradient predicts how the return actually changes. The policies are the trained Ant and Go1 policies from Section 6 (both trained with explicit Euler at 2 ms) and the Humanoid policy from the longest reference-reward run of Section 7, which dives forward and falls. Each is evaluated in every simulator configuration from states reached after 1 s (Humanoid: 0.2 s) of its own motion, over two sets of start states.

Up to 8 control steps (0.16 s), every configuration gives consistent gradients for Ant (cosine ≥ 0.78, slope ratio 0.9–1.7). Beyond that, the timestep dominates. At 16 steps, the Ant cosine is 0.95 at 2 ms, 0.88 at 5 ms, 0.56 at 10 ms, and 0.48 at 20 ms, and the slope ratio grows from 1.6–2.0 at 2 ms to 3.0 at 10 ms and 4.6 at 20 ms. The Go1 contrast is sharper: at 2 ms the gradient stays consistent (cosine ≥ 0.97) with a slope ratio of 1.0 all the way to 32 steps, whereas at 20 ms the cosine is 0.41 at 8 steps and near zero at 16. For Ant and Go1, explicit and implicit integration at 2 ms are indistinguishable. The Humanoid states visited by its SHAC policy, which are dominated by falls and impacts, give the shortest usable horizon: at 4 steps the cosine is 0.84–0.89 at 2 ms, 0.74 at 5 ms, and 0.52 at 10 ms, and at 8 steps it is between 0.20 and 0.65 in every configuration. A randomly initialized Humanoid policy near standing shows the same ordering over longer horizons: consistent gradients to about 16 steps at 2 ms (cosine 0.49–0.74 across start states), to about 8 steps at 10 ms, and fewer at 20 ms.

What does not explain the timestep effect

Two plausible mechanisms were tested on the Humanoid at 10 ms with a 16-step horizon. Softening all contacts and joint limits, raising their constraint time constant from 20 ms to 50 or 100 ms so that each constraint response spans several physics steps, did not restore consistency (cosine 0.09 and 0.15). Increasing the solver budget fourfold (400 iterations, 200 line-search iterations), which would help if the implicit-function-theorem adjoint suffered from unconverged solves, did not change the result (cosine −0.08). Pyramidal and elliptic friction cones gave the same gradient quality. One remaining explanation, which these experiments do not confirm, is that contact sets, stick–slip states, and limit activations are fixed once per physics step: with a larger step, each such event changes the discrete dynamics by a larger jump, and the local gradient misses more of the true change.

Consequence for training

The SHAC window \(T\) must stay inside the horizon over which gradients are informative. On Ant at 10 ms, three otherwise identical training runs reached a mean episode speed of 0.08 m/s with \(T = 32\), 0.30 m/s with \(T = 16\), and 0.72 m/s with \(T = 8\) after 200 epochs (the \(T = 32\) run used a 1000-epoch learning-rate schedule instead of 1500). All training in Section 6 therefore uses \(T = 8\), which is informative for Ant and Go1 in every configuration, so that the configurations differ only in the simulator.

A standard remedy from recurrent-network training also helps the gradient here. Capping the norm of each world's state adjoint at three times the batch median before it is passed to the previous step keeps the Humanoid half-batch cosine at 0.65–0.70 for horizons of 32 and 64 steps, whereas the unclipped cosine at 32 steps is 0.03–0.48. The capped gradient is biased: its slope is 2–3 times the finite-difference slope, but it has the same sign. Section 7 reports how this affects training.

6. Training quadrupeds

SHAC learned Ant and Go1 locomotion from scratch in every simulator configuration. Per training epoch, small timesteps learned as well or better, and only small-step policies transferred reliably to the fine reference simulator.

Ant and Go1 were each trained from scratch with SHAC in five simulator configurations: explicit Euler at 2 ms (the integrator of the earlier study) and implicitfast at 2, 5, 10, and 20 ms. Each configuration ran three seeds for 1000 epochs of 256 environments and 8 control steps, that is, 2.05 million control steps or 11.4 hours of simulated time per run. Only the simulator differs between configurations. No run skipped an update because of a non-finite or exploding gradient.

Per epoch, the 2 ms configurations produced the best policies, and explicit and implicit integration at 2 ms gave similar results (Ant: 6.26 ± 0.06 and 6.37 ± 0.10 m/s; Go1: 1.58 ± 0.06 and 1.56 ± 0.08 m/s in the reference simulator). For Ant, the final reference-simulator speed fell from 6.3–6.4 m/s at 2 ms to 5.2 m/s at 5 ms, 2.7 m/s at 10 ms, and 3.5 m/s at 20 ms, which matches the ordering of gradient quality in Section 5. For Go1, whose reward saturates at 1.5 m/s, the 2, 5, and 10 ms policies all reached the target and walked with the same speed, survival, and lateral drift in the reference simulator as in their training simulator.

The 20 ms Go1 policies did not transfer. They moved at 1.55 ± 0.47 m/s in their training simulator but only 0.84 ± 0.55 m/s in the reference simulator, with 2.6 m of lateral drift instead of 0.1 m. In the reference simulator, the seed-0 policy settles into a crouch and barely moves (Figure 7). These policies exploit motion that exists only at the coarse timestep. The Ant policies trained at 10 and 20 ms were slower but did not lose performance in the reference simulator; their survival was even higher there (89–95%) than in the training simulator (60%), which indicates that the coarse simulators made the Ant flip more easily rather than giving it an advantage.

Equal wall-clock budget

A 10 ms configuration performs one-fifth of the physics steps of a 2 ms configuration per epoch, so for the same wall-clock time it can train for more epochs. Two seeds per robot were trained at 10 ms for 4000 epochs, which took 1.8 (Ant) and 1.7 (Go1) times the isolated wall-clock time of a 1000-epoch implicitfast run at 2 ms. In the training simulator this closes most of the Ant gap: 5.61 ± 0.63 m/s after 4000 epochs, against 6.10–6.19 m/s for the 2 ms runs after 1000. In the reference simulator, however, these policies reach only 2.66 ± 0.26 m/s, no more than the 1000-epoch 10 ms policies (2.72 ± 0.14 m/s). The additional speed exists only at the coarse timestep. For Go1, the longer 10 ms runs did not improve on the 1000-epoch runs (1.44 ± 0.03 against 1.56 ± 0.01 m/s in the reference simulator) but still transferred, because the task saturates at its target speed within 1000 epochs.

7. Humanoids

With the SHAC reference tasks, no tested configuration learned humanoid balance. The diagnostics point to short informative gradient horizons on falling states and to the inability of local gradients to value contact-mode changes, rather than to the integrator or the critic. Section 8 identifies further problems of these task setups (penetrating initial states, height-based termination, and, for H1, self-collision pairs without adjoint support) and shows that a differentiable gait prior on the retuned H1 task changes the outcome.

Outcome

The MuJoCo Humanoid falls in 0.66–0.82 s when all actuators are idle. SHAC was run for 81 to 741 epochs in seventeen configurations: the reference reward at 2 and 10 ms, with 8, 16, and 32-step windows, 256 or 512 environments, stronger actuators matching the reference Humanoid (1.75× gear), a pure standing task without the velocity term, lower learning rates, a faster critic, reduced reset noise, softer contacts (including a 100 ms contact time constant that approximates the compliant penalty contact of the reference SHAC simulator), additional uprightness and angular-rate terms, per-world adjoint clipping, and full-episode back-propagation. In every case the mean episode length ended between 0.77 and 1.21 s. The policies slow the fall slightly but never balance. With the velocity term, they converge to a forward dive: the body stiffens and topples forward to collect velocity reward before termination (Figure 8).

Diagnostics

Several explanations were ruled out. The critic is informative: its value estimate has a rank correlation of 0.74–0.99 with the remaining time before a fall. The step adjoint itself is accurate (Section 5), but on the fall-dominated states that the Humanoid policies visit, the policy gradient stays consistent for only about four control steps, fewer than for the quadrupeds. The contact model is not frictionless: foot contacts are three-dimensional with a friction coefficient of 1. Physical feasibility is not in question either: this is the classic humanoid model distributed with MuJoCo, which model-free reinforcement learning routinely trains to stand and walk.

The simulator's gradients do contain balance information over longer horizons. Open-loop trajectory optimization, which gives each of 64 worlds its own 2 s action sequence and back-propagates through all 100 control steps, raised the mean time upright from 0.72 s to 1.38 s and kept 28% of worlds standing for the full 2 s after 120 Adam iterations. Truncating the back-propagation to 16 steps slowed this progress. A shared feedback policy trained with back-propagation through complete 1 s episodes, however, did not improve.

A humanoid cannot stand still by stiffening its joints. For H1, holding the body upright as an inverted pendulum about the ankle would require an ankle stiffness near \(mgl \approx 500\) N·m/rad, where \(m\) is the mass, \(g\) gravitational acceleration, and \(l\) the height of the center of mass. H1's single ankle pitch joint provides at most 40 N·m. No constant joint-target pose stood for more than 1.5 s. Balance therefore requires stepping, that is, lifting a foot and placing it elsewhere. Stepping changes the set of active contacts. The implicit-function-theorem adjoint differentiates the solution for the current contact set, so it cannot represent the benefit of a contact that does not exist yet. The quadrupeds are statically stable on four feet and can improve by pushing harder or more efficiently within their current contact pattern, which the gradient does capture.

Position servos and a gait clock

Modern humanoid controllers usually command joint targets tracked by proportional-derivative servos. H1-PD replaces H1's torque motors with position servos (\(k_p\) = 40–300 N·m/rad, \(k_v\) = 2–6 N·m·s/rad, torque limits equal to the original motor ranges); its idle fall time increases from 0.5 s to 1.3 s. The servo damping is an actuator velocity gain, the case in which only implicitfast integrates it implicitly. A gait clock gives the policy a 0.8 s phase signal, and a reward term pulls the hip and knee pitch toward an alternating stepping pattern, so that stepping is encouraged by a smooth, state-dependent term rather than discovered through the gradient. After 281 epochs with 512 environments at 5 ms, the policy followed the stepping pattern, and the reward per step rose from 2.0 to 3.0. Episodes still lasted only 1.22 s, no longer than those of the idle robot. Section 8 replaces the joint-angle pattern by a prior on foot heights and foot placement, and combines it with Isaac Lab's servo gains, torso-contact termination, and floor-only collisions; with these changes H1 learns to walk.

8. Retuning the environments and the trainer

A second study modified the tasks to follow Isaac Lab's locomotion environments, reduced the trainer's cost, and tested task and gradient-estimator changes. A differentiable gait prior with a foot-placement term made the Unitree H1 humanoid learnable with SHAC, and a faster target critic let the G1 learn to balance; on an Isaac-Lab-style Go1 task, SHAC usually converged to standing still.

Procedure

The study proceeded in iterations. Each iteration formed hypotheses from the previous results, from the environment definitions of Isaac Lab, NVIDIA's robot-learning framework, and from the literature on first-order policy optimization, for example SHAC and its adaptive-horizon successor [Georgiev et al. 2024]. It then implemented one change per hypothesis and screened it with training runs of 400 to 1500 epochs. Screening runs shared the GPU with up to two other runs, so their wall-clock times are not comparable; costs are instead reported from separate timing runs in which the GPU partition ran nothing else. The hardware is one Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) partition, a quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, with four CPU cores. Policies were evaluated in the reference simulator of Section 6 (implicitfast, 1 ms). Table 13 at the end of this section lists the iterations.

Environment changes

The velocity-tracking tasks follow Isaac Lab's flat-terrain locomotion environments for Go1, H1, and G1. The policy observes the base linear and angular velocity in the body frame, the direction of gravity in the body frame, the velocity command \(c = (c_x, c_y, c_\omega)\) of forward, lateral, and yaw velocity, the joint positions relative to a default pose \(q_0\), the joint velocities, and the previous action. An action \(a\) sets the targets \(q_\text{target} = q_0 + s_a \tanh(a)\) of proportional-derivative servos, with action scale \(s_a = 0.5\) rad. The rewards use Isaac Lab's terms and weights. Velocity tracking is rewarded by the kernels \(\exp(-\lVert c_{xy} - v_{xy}\rVert^2/\sigma_c^2)\) for the planar base velocity \(v_{xy}\) and \(\exp(-(c_\omega - \omega_z)^2/\sigma_c^2)\) for the yaw rate \(\omega_z\), with \(\sigma_c = 0.5\) (m/s or rad/s). Penalties act on vertical velocity, roll and pitch rates, tilt, torques, joint accelerations, action changes, and joint-limit violations, and a foot air-time term rewards steps. Isaac Lab multiplies every reward by the control interval; the tasks here do not, which scales all rewards equally. Commands are resampled every 10 s, and episodes last 20 s (1000 control steps).

Four changes to the simulated robots and resets correct problems of the earlier setup that affect the analytic gradient or the initial states. Their individual effects were not isolated:

  • Servo models. Isaac Lab's implicit proportional-derivative drives become MuJoCo position servos with force \(\tau = \operatorname{clip}(k_p (q_\text{target} - q) - k_v \dot q, \pm\tau_\text{max})\). The force limit is set on the actuator, where the adjoint's saturation handling applies, and implicitfast integrates the \(k_v\) term implicitly. H1 uses Isaac Lab's gains and default pose: \(k_p = 150\)–\(200\) N·m/rad with \(k_v = 5\) N·m·s/rad on hips, knees, and torso, and \(k_p = 20\) N·m/rad with \(k_v = 4\) N·m·s/rad on the ankles.
  • Floor-only collisions. The adjoint supports eleven primitive collision pairs. Other pairs, such as capsule–cylinder and cylinder–cylinder, contribute no gradient without a warning. The Menagerie Go1 and H1 models contain 247 and 127 such self-collision pairs. Like Isaac Lab, which disables self-collision for these robots, the retuned tasks let robot geometry collide only with the floor.
  • Termination by torso contact. Isaac Lab ends a humanoid episode only when the torso touches the ground, with a penalty of 200. All humanoid configurations of Section 7 terminated at a torso height of 0.6–0.74 m instead. The retuned humanoid tasks detect torso–floor contact from the simulator's contact list.
  • Physical initial states. The reset used in Section 7, which follows the SHAC reference Humanoid, places 50% of the MuJoCo Humanoid's initial states in floor penetration of up to 14 cm and 23% in self-penetration, which produces large impulses at the start of an episode. Initial states are now drawn from a bank of states checked for penetration in advance, with Isaac Lab's randomization: random heading, base velocities of up to 0.5 m/s and 0.5 rad/s per axis, and, for Go1, joint positions scaled by a factor between 0.5 and 1.5.

A differentiable gait prior

The analytic gradient treats the contact set as fixed within a step, so it cannot value a foot that is about to lift off or touch down. The pull request's own Go1 trajectory-optimization example addresses this with a reference trajectory for the foot heights. The same idea gives reward terms that are differentiable with respect to the joint positions. A gait clock with period \(P = 0.8\) s assigns foot \(i\) the phase \(\phi_i(t) = (t/P + \delta_i) \bmod 1\), with offsets \(\delta = (0, 0.5)\) for the two feet. A foot is in stance while \(\phi_i < D\), with duty factor \(D = 0.6\), and otherwise in swing with progress \(\rho_i = (\phi_i - D)/(1 - D) \in [0, 1)\). Let \(p_i\) be the position of a point under the sole of foot \(i\), computed by differentiable forward kinematics from the joint positions, and let \([\cdot]\) equal 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. Three terms are added to the reward:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_\text{gait} = &-w_h \sum_i \big(p_{i,z} - z_0 - h_\text{sw} \sin(\pi \rho_i)\,[\phi_i \ge D]\big)^2 \\ &- w_s \sum_{i:\,\phi_i < D} \Big\lVert \tfrac{\Delta p_{i,xy}}{\Delta t_c} \Big\rVert^2 \\ &- w_r \sum_{i:\,\rho_i \ge 0.5} \big\lVert \tilde p_{i,xy} - \bar p_i - \kappa_v v_b - \kappa_e (v_b - c_{xy}) \big\rVert^2 . \end{aligned} \]

The first term asks the foot to follow a swing arc of height \(h_\text{sw} = 0.08\) m above its standing height \(z_0\). The second penalizes the horizontal speed of feet that the clock places in stance, where \(\Delta p\) is the displacement over one control interval \(\Delta t_c = 20\) ms. The third is a Raibert-type foot-placement rule for the second half of the swing. Here \(\tilde p_{i,xy}\) is the foot position relative to the base in the heading frame (the frame rotated with the base's yaw), \(\bar p_i\) its position in the default pose, \(v_b\) the base's planar velocity in the heading frame, and \(\kappa_v = \kappa_e = 0.1\) s. Placing the foot ahead in proportion to the velocity arrests a fall, and the term in \(v_b - c_{xy}\) shifts the step to accelerate or brake. The weights are \(w_h = 40\), \(w_s = 2\), and \(w_r = 10\) (in m−2 or s2/m2). The policy observes \(\sin(2\pi t/P)\) and \(\cos(2\pi t/P)\). The prior prescribes when each foot should be in the air and, through the placement rule, where it should land; the gradient decides how the joints achieve this and how the body balances.

Trainer changes and cost

The trainer was rewritten without changing the SHAC update. Three changes reduce its cost:

  • No host synchronization in the rollout. Resets and non-finite states are handled with masked tensor operations, and episode statistics accumulate on the GPU, so the CPU does not wait for the GPU inside an epoch.
  • A captured critic update. The 64 minibatch steps of the critic regression (16 passes over 4 minibatches) are captured as one CUDA graph, which shortens a Go1 epoch from 119 to 98 ms (Table 11).
  • Stored forward steps. The backward pass of Section 2 recomputes every physics step before applying its adjoint. The step adjoint needs only the step's input and its completed forward data, so the trainer now keeps one simulator state buffer per physics step of the window, each with its own captured forward and backward graph, and calls the adjoint directly. On identical inputs, the gradients agree with the recomputing backward to within the run-to-run variation of the GPU's contact detection: a relative difference of \(4 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for H1, and 1.2% for Go1, where two recomputing runs differ by 1.4%.

9. Limitations

  • Integrator coverage. For all standard models tested, joint damping is the only velocity-dependent smooth force, so implicitfast and Euler with implicit damping are identical. Go1-kv, H1-PD, and the servo-driven robots of Section 8 exercise the additional terms of implicitfast, but only Go1-kv was compared quantitatively against Euler.
  • Stability protocol. Divergence was measured under vigorous random actuation from near-standing states. Trained policies visit a narrower set of states, so the stable timestep for a given policy can be larger; the 20 ms training runs themselves encountered no non-finite states.
  • Accuracy is local. Integration error was measured over one 20 ms control interval. Long-horizon trajectory differences between timesteps are dominated by chaotic divergence and were not reported.
  • Tuning. SHAC hyperparameters follow the reference implementation except for the window length and the environment count. They were not tuned per configuration, and a configuration-specific window, such as \(T = 16\) at 2 ms, might change the ranking in Section 6.
  • Humanoid result. The negative result of Section 7 covers the variants in Table 7, each trained for at most 741 epochs. The H1 walking policies of Section 8 depend on a gait prior that prescribes when each foot is in the air; they do not show that SHAC discovers stepping without such a prior. Except for the three-seed row of Table 8, the H1 results (the other rows of Table 8, Table 9, and Figure 10) come from one training seed. Only the foot-placement term of the prior was ablated.
  • Task fidelity. The retuned tasks follow Isaac Lab's definitions but omit its observation noise, mass randomization, pushes, and heading controller, and the Go1 servo model approximates Isaac Lab's learned actuator network.
  • Adjoint limitations of the tested commit. The step adjoint does not account for actuator force saturation in the control derivative (corrected in the harness), does not support mesh collisions, tendons, or connect and weld equality constraints, and ships without tests at this commit. Collision pairs outside its eleven supported primitive pairs contribute no gradient without a warning. For the Euler integrator with implicit joint damping, the derivative of the next velocity with respect to the joint positions omits the configuration dependence of the mass matrix; for a two-link pendulum with joint damping 2 and 20, its relative error against finite differences is 56% and 430%. The training runs in this report used implicitfast or explicit Euler, which are not affected.
  • Hardware. All timings come from one MIG 1g.24gb instance, a quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, with four CPU cores. Kernel-launch overhead is hidden by graph replay, but the relative cost of learner updates versus simulation depends on the machine and the batch size.

10. Conclusion

At commit 357a75d, the MuJoCo Warp step adjoint supports implicit integration, and implicit treatment of damping does allow larger timesteps. It removes the explicit-damping divergence that limited the earlier configuration to about 10 ms for Go1 and 5 ms for the Humanoid. For the standard models, MuJoCo's default Euler integrator already provides this benefit; implicitfast adds accuracy only when damping is part of the actuator, as for position servos with a derivative gain. Implicit integration does not make 20 ms safe for humanoids under vigorous actuation.

Larger timesteps raise simulator throughput almost in proportion to the step up to 10 ms (4.8–4.9 times from 2 to 10 ms with gradients), and less beyond that for contact-rich robots. End to end, a SHAC epoch at 10 ms is only 2.3–2.7 times faster than at 2 ms, because learner costs are fixed. Capturing the forward and backward steps as CUDA graphs is an independent speedup of 2.7–7.8 times over launching kernels from Python.

Larger timesteps degrade what SHAC needs most, the gradient over a rollout. The horizon over which the analytic policy gradient remains consistent shrinks as the timestep grows, from at least 32 control steps (the longest measured) at 2 ms to about 4 at 20 ms for Go1. Per epoch, Ant learned more slowly at large timesteps. Speed gained by training at 20 ms (Go1) or by training longer at 10 ms (Ant) did not survive transfer to a fine-step simulator, because at these timesteps the simulated dynamics differ measurably from the converged ones.

In the simulator configurations of Section 6, SHAC trained Ant and Go1 locomotion from scratch on the analytic adjoint, provided the window stayed within the informative horizon (8 control steps). With the reference tasks of Section 7, it did not learn humanoid balance. The evidence points to two structural limitations: on the falling states a learning humanoid visits, the analytic gradient stays informative for only about four control steps, and balance requires stepping, which changes the contact set in a way that a local gradient taken within the current contact set cannot value.

Modifying the tasks to follow Isaac Lab and adding a differentiable gait prior with a foot-placement rule made the Unitree H1 humanoid learnable with SHAC. After 1500 epochs, an estimated 9.3 minutes of training with the GPU partition otherwise idle, the policies of three seeds survive 99.5% of 10 s rollouts in the fine-step reference simulator and track randomized velocity commands with a mean planar error of 0.15 ± 0.02 m/s; the seed-0 policy also follows fixed forward and turning commands. Without the foot-placement term the prior did not produce walking. For the Unitree G1, the same prior with Isaac Lab's servo gains produced balance but not walking, and only after the target critic was made to follow the trained critic faster than DiffRL's humanoid setting. A plausible interpretation is that the placement rule supplies the value of a future foot contact, which the analytic gradient cannot represent, while the gradient shapes how the joints produce the steps and how the robot balances. Keeping each physics step's forward data in memory removes the recomputation in the backward pass and shortens epochs by 1.3–2.6 times with gradients equal to within run-to-run variation. On an Isaac-Lab-style Go1 task, standing still was a strong local optimum: SHAC, and PPO with the same bounded action range, converged to it, whereas PPO with Isaac Lab's unbounded actions learned the task; with the original Menagerie Go1, SHAC walked with only some of four seeds.

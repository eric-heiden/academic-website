SHAC locomotion in MuJoCo Warp: implicit integration and retuned tasks

Timestep, throughput, gradient quality, training outcomes, and a retuning study with the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp pull request #1535

Simulator
MuJoCo Warp PR #1535 at 357a75d
Software
Warp 1.15.0 · MuJoCo 3.12.1.dev · PyTorch 2.11.0
Hardware
One quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU (Multi-Instance GPU partition 1g.24gb) · 4 CPU cores

Abstract

Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) trains control policies by back-propagating short simulated rollouts through a differentiable simulator. This report evaluates SHAC on the analytic step adjoint of MuJoCo Warp pull request #1535, which at commit 357a75d supports implicit time integration. It asks whether implicit integration allows larger physics timesteps, whether larger timesteps raise useful training throughput, and whether quadruped and humanoid locomotion can be learned from scratch. Treating joint damping implicitly removes the explicit-damping instability that limited earlier configurations (Go1 diverges at 20 ms and the MuJoCo Humanoid at 10 ms with explicit damping). For the tested robots, MuJoCo's default Euler integrator already provides this benefit, and implicitfast adds accuracy only for damping inside the actuators. Going from 2 to 10 ms raises simulator throughput with gradients by 4.8–4.9 times but shortens complete SHAC epochs by only 2.3–2.7 times. Larger timesteps, however, shorten the horizon over which the analytic policy gradient stays informative, from at least 32 control steps at 2 ms to about 4 at 20 ms for Go1. They also change the simulated dynamics enough that policies trained at 10–20 ms can fail to transfer to a fine-step simulator. With the reference tasks, SHAC learned Ant and Go1 locomotion from scratch in every tested simulator configuration. The best Ant policies came from 2 ms training, and Go1 policies trained at up to 10 ms transferred to a fine-step simulator without loss. With these tasks it did not learn balance for the MuJoCo Humanoid in any of seventeen configurations, nor for a position-controlled Unitree H1 with a joint-angle gait clock; the diagnostics point to the inability of local gradients to value contact changes such as a new foot placement. Section 8 shows how task changes, a gait prior, joint damping, and a faster target critic relax this limit.

That part modifies the tasks to follow Isaac Lab's locomotion environments and reduces the trainer's cost. Keeping each physics step's forward data in memory removes the recomputation in the backward pass; with further changes, an epoch of the Section 6 Ant and Go1 configurations takes 1.75 times less time at 2 ms. With Isaac Lab's H1 task definition and a differentiable gait prior on foot heights and foot placement, SHAC learned Unitree H1 walking policies. With a target critic that follows the trained critic faster than in the SHAC reference humanoid settings (Polyak factor 0.9 instead of 0.995), 600 epochs gave 99.3 ± 0.8% survival over 10 s and a mean velocity-tracking error of 0.14 ± 0.01 m/s over three seeds in a 1 ms reference simulator. At the measured epoch time, these 600 epochs take about 2.3 minutes. Reusing the stored action gradients for four further policy updates per epoch, as in reparameterization proximal policy optimization, reached a fixed training-quality threshold in about 40% fewer epochs at the same epoch cost; with a 10 ms timestep and 1024 environments it reached the threshold after 0.6–0.7 minutes of training. Without the foot-placement term the prior did not produce walking. With stronger prior weights, the Unitree G1 learned to walk with two of three seeds; with the H1 weights it only balanced. The faster target critic together with the joint damping of the original SHAC simulator let the MuJoCo Humanoid learn to run without any prior (2.1 m/s, 89% survival over three seeds); either change alone did not. On the Isaac Lab Go1 task, SHAC usually converged to standing still, whereas the SHAC reference reward of the first part, whose forward-velocity term grows linearly up to a cap, trained two Go1 models to walk (one seed each), and proximal policy optimization (PPO) with Isaac Lab's action scale of 0.25 rad learned the task. The analytic adjoint occasionally gives a single simulated world a gradient 10 to more than 100 times the median over worlds. Capping each world's gradient at 10 times the median made all four seeds walk on the forward-command variant of the Isaac Lab Go1 task, against none of the four seeds without the cap; with lateral and yaw-rate commands, no seed learned with 512 environments, and one of four learned with 2048 environments and 1600 epochs. Command sets with a single direction of motion were learned, but SHAC stood whenever the commanded direction varied.

1. Background and question

Analytic simulator gradients can train policies directly, but only if the gradients stay informative over the planning horizon and the simulator is fast enough to generate millions of transitions.

Short-horizon actor-critic

Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) [Xu et al. 2022] trains a policy \(\pi_\theta\) by differentiating simulated returns with respect to the policy parameters \(\theta\). Each training epoch rolls out \(N\) parallel environments for \(T\) control steps from the states reached in the previous epoch and minimizes

\[L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t} r_t^{n} + \gamma^{T} V_\phi\!\left(s_T^{n}\right)\right],\]

where \(r_t^n\) is the reward of environment \(n\) at control step \(t\), \(\gamma = 0.99\) is the discount factor, \(s_T^n\) is the state at the end of the window, and \(V_\phi\) is a learned value function (the critic) with parameters \(\phi\). The gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\) is obtained by back-propagating through the policy and through every simulation step in the window. The critic summarizes everything beyond the window; it is trained separately on temporal-difference targets with \(\lambda = 0.95\). If an environment terminates inside the window (for example because the robot falls), its sum stops there and the value term is zero. A longer window exposes more of the consequences of an action to the gradient, but back-propagating through contact-rich dynamics over many steps can produce gradients that are large and inconsistent, and this effect is the main practical limit on \(T\).

The MuJoCo Warp adjoint

MuJoCo Warp (MJWarp) is a GPU implementation of MuJoCo. Pull request #1535 adds an analytic backward pass for one simulation step. MuJoCo computes accelerations by solving a convex constraint problem for contacts, joint limits, and friction; the pull request differentiates the converged solution with the implicit function theorem instead of differentiating the solver iterations. At the commit tested here, the backward pass supports the Newton constraint solver, elliptic and pyramidal friction cones, eleven primitive collision pairs (plane with sphere, capsule, ellipsoid, cylinder, or box; sphere with sphere, box, capsule, or cylinder; capsule–capsule; and capsule–box; other pairs contribute no gradient), and two integrators: Euler and implicitfast. An earlier SHAC study on this pull request, archived here, used an older commit, explicit time integration, and hybrid policies that were first trained with other methods.

Time integration

MuJoCo advances generalized positions \(q\) and velocities \(v\) with a timestep \(h\). All integrators considered here are semi-implicit: the new velocity is used to update the position, \(q_{k+1} = q_k \oplus h\,v_{k+1}\), where \(\oplus\) denotes integration on the configuration manifold (quaternions for free joints). They differ in how the acceleration used for \(v_{k+1} = v_k + h\,a_k\) is computed:

\[\underbrace{M a_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)}_{\text{explicit damping}},\qquad \underbrace{(M + hB)\,a_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)}_{\text{implicit joint damping}},\qquad \underbrace{\left(M - h\,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}\right) a_k = f(q_k, v_k, u_k)}_{\texttt{implicitfast}}.\]

Here \(M\) is the joint-space inertia matrix, \(f\) collects all applied, passive, bias, and constraint forces, \(u_k\) is the actuator control, and \(B\) is the diagonal matrix of joint damping coefficients. MuJoCo's Euler integrator uses the middle form by default; disabling its eulerdamp option gives the explicit form on the left, which is the configuration of the earlier study. implicitfast additionally treats all velocity-dependent smooth forces implicitly, including actuator velocity gains such as the derivative gain of a position servo, while omitting Coriolis and centrifugal derivatives. A robot whose only velocity-dependent force is joint damping is therefore integrated identically by the last two forms.

For a single joint with effective inertia \(I\) and damping coefficient \(b\), the explicit form multiplies the velocity by \(1 - hb/I\) in each step when no other force acts. The update is unstable when

\[h > h_{\text{crit}} = \frac{2 I}{b}.\]

Joints with small inertia and large damping, such as the lower legs of a quadruped or the limbs of a humanoid, therefore bound the usable timestep of explicit integration. In this report \(I\) is the effective inertia \(1/(M^{-1})_{ii}\) of joint \(i\) at the nominal pose.

Question

The study asks whether implicit integration in the current pull request allows larger simulation timesteps, whether larger timesteps increase useful training throughput, and whether SHAC trained on the analytic adjoint learns locomotion for quadrupeds and humanoids from scratch. A second part (Section 8) modifies the tasks to follow the locomotion environments of Isaac Lab, NVIDIA's robot-learning framework, reduces the cost of the trainer, and asks which changes make humanoid locomotion learnable and training cheaper.

Five robot models, a fixed 20 ms control interval, and a training harness that replays the simulator's forward and backward steps as captured GPU graphs.

Go1-kv is identical to Go1 except that the joint damping is moved into the position servos as an actuator velocity gain \(k_v\). Many robot descriptions model the derivative gain this way. Control and physics timesteps

The policy acts every \(\Delta t_c = 20\) ms and holds its control constant over \(K = \Delta t_c / h\) physics steps. The physics timestep \(h\) ranges from 1 to 20 ms, so \(K\) ranges from 20 to 1. The training objective and all evaluation metrics are defined on the 20 ms control grid, which makes configurations with different \(h\) directly comparable.

Training harness

The harness follows the checkpointing scheme of the pull request's own differentiable-simulation examples. One physics step and its analytic backward pass are each captured once as a CUDA graph. During a rollout, the forward graph is replayed \(K\) times per control step while the step inputs (positions, velocities, and the solver's warm start) are saved. During back-propagation, the saved inputs are restored in reverse order and the backward graph is replayed, which recomputes the step and applies the analytic adjoint. PyTorch and Warp share one CUDA stream, so no host synchronization is needed between the policy and the simulator (Figure 1).

Two corrections were needed on top of the pull request. First, the adjoint of the control input ignores actuator force limits: it returns a nonzero derivative even when a servo is saturated and its force no longer depends on the control. The harness zeroes the control derivative of saturated actuators, using the actuator force recomputed during the backward replay. Second, the upstream adjoint tests were removed in the refactor that produced this commit, so gradient correctness was checked independently against finite differences (Section 5).

SHAC configuration

The SHAC implementation follows the reference code: a tanh-squashed Gaussian policy with a learned, state-independent log standard deviation initialized to −1; multilayer perceptrons with ELU activations and layer normalization; observation normalization frozen during each rollout; a TD(\(\lambda\)) critic with 16 passes over 4 minibatches per epoch; Adam with \(\beta = (0.7, 0.95)\); learning rates decayed linearly to \(10^{-5}\); and gradient-norm clipping at 1. The value at the window end and the critic targets come from a target critic with parameters \(\phi_\text{target}\), updated once per epoch as \(\phi_\text{target} \leftarrow \alpha\,\phi_\text{target} + (1 - \alpha)\,\phi\); a larger Polyak factor \(\alpha\) makes the target follow the critic more slowly. The factor is 0.2 for Ant, as in the reference code, and for Go1, and 0.995 for the humanoids, as for the reference Humanoid. Training uses 256 environments and 1000 epochs. The window length \(T\) is 8 control steps (0.16 s) unless stated otherwise; Section 5 explains this choice.

Observations contain the torso height, orientation, and velocities, the joint positions and scaled velocities, the uprightness and heading projections, and the previous action. Rewards follow the reference Ant and Humanoid tasks: forward velocity, plus \(0.1\) times uprightness, plus heading alignment, plus a height term, minus an action penalty for humanoids. Episodes last 1000 control steps (20 s) and end early when the torso falls below a height threshold. The reference reward caused the Go1 to gallop at about 4 m/s with extreme hip flexion, so the Go1 velocity term is capped at a target of 1.5 m/s, and lateral velocity and action changes are penalized. Resets perturb the root position by up to ±0.1 m, the orientation by up to 7.5°, the joint angles by up to ±0.2 rad, and all velocities by up to ±0.25.

Evaluation

Each trained policy is evaluated deterministically (tanh of the Gaussian mean) for 20 s from 256 randomized starts, both in its training simulator and in a common reference simulator: implicitfast with \(h = 1\) ms, which is finer than every training configuration. A policy that depends on artifacts of a large timestep loses performance in the reference simulator. Reported metrics are the survival fraction (episodes without a fall), the forward speed (forward displacement divided by the time survived), and the lateral drift.

3. Integrator stability and accuracy

Implicit damping removes the explicit-damping stability limit, but it does not remove other limits, and the one-step error grows linearly with the timestep for every integrator.

Protocol

For each model, integrator, and timestep \(h \in \{1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20\}\) ms, 1024 worlds start from the nominal pose with small random joint and velocity perturbations and receive the same temporally correlated random actions for 10 s. Actions follow \(u_t = \operatorname{clip}(0.9\,u_{t-1} + 0.45\,\xi_t, -1, 1)\) with standard normal \(\xi_t\), a vigorous excitation that makes most robots fall and flail. A world is counted as diverged when its state becomes non-finite, a generalized velocity exceeds \(10^3\), or the root leaves the height band from −1 to 20 m.

Accuracy is measured locally. States are sampled at four time points from a reference rollout (implicitfast, \(h = 0.25\) ms), giving 4096 samples per model. Each sample is advanced by one 20 ms control interval with the sampled action, in the reference and in every tested configuration. The reported error is the median over samples of the root-mean-square joint-angle difference after that interval. Repeating the reference step with \(h = 0.125\) ms changes the result by at most 0.05° and 0.03 mm, far below the differences reported here.

4. Throughput

Graph replay and a larger timestep each make the simulator several times faster. Larger timesteps shorten SHAC epochs by only a factor of two to three, because the learner and per-control-step costs do not shrink.

All timings in this section were taken with the GPU otherwise idle. Simulator throughput is reported as world-steps per second, the number of parallel worlds times the number of physics steps completed per second. A training configuration needs \(K = 20\text{ ms}/h\) physics steps per control step, so for a fixed cost per physics step, simulated time per wall-clock second grows in proportion to \(h\).

5. Gradient quality

The analytic gradients agree with finite differences where the dynamics are smooth. How far they stay informative along a rollout depends strongly on the physics timestep: larger steps shorten the usable horizon.

Correctness of the step adjoint

For 64 worlds per model, the directional derivative of a five-control-step objective with respect to the control sequence was compared with central finite differences, world by world. With perturbation size \(\varepsilon = 0.01\), the analytic value is within 5% of the finite difference in 73% (Humanoid) to 89% (Go1) of worlds, with median relative errors of 0.05–0.3%. In the remaining worlds the finite difference does not converge as \(\varepsilon\) decreases, which identifies a discontinuity inside the perturbation interval, such as a contact activating, a stick–slip transition, or a joint limit engaging. A contact-free, zero-gravity Ant without joint limits matches finite differences to three or four digits over 4 control steps and matches the smallest-perturbation finite difference over 16 steps; enabling joint limits introduces a gap, because the analytic gradient describes only the local dynamics and does not include the jump caused by a limit activating.

Policy gradients along a rollout

SHAC needs the gradient of a multi-step return with respect to the policy parameters. For a fixed policy and a fixed batch of 256 start states, define

\[J_T(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t^n\,\chi_t^n,\]

where the alive indicator \(\chi_t^n \in \{0, 1\}\) is 1 until environment \(n\) terminates and 0 afterwards, so it stops the sum at termination. Two diagnostics are reported. The half-batch cosine is the cosine similarity between \(\nabla_\theta J_T\) computed on two disjoint halves of the batch. It is near 1 when the gradient direction is a property of the policy and the task, and near 0 when a few chaotic or discontinuous rollouts dominate. The slope ratio divides the analytic directional derivative along the normalized gradient by the finite-difference derivative, which is the median over three perturbation sizes. It is near 1 when the analytic gradient predicts how the return actually changes. The policies are the trained Ant and Go1 policies from Section 6 (both trained with explicit Euler at 2 ms) and the Humanoid policy after 450 epochs of the 512-environment reference-reward run of Section 7 (2 ms, \(T = 16\); Table 7), which dives forward and falls. Each is evaluated in every simulator configuration from states reached after 1 s (Humanoid: 0.2 s) of its own motion, over two sets of start states.

Up to 8 control steps (0.16 s), every configuration gives consistent gradients for Ant (cosine ≥ 0.75, slope ratio 0.9–1.7). Beyond that, the timestep dominates. At 16 steps, the Ant cosine is 0.95 at 2 ms, 0.88 at 5 ms, 0.56 at 10 ms, and 0.48 at 20 ms, and the slope ratio grows from 1.6–2.0 at 2 ms to 3.0 at 10 ms and 4.6 at 20 ms. The Go1 contrast is sharper: at 2 ms the gradient stays consistent (cosine ≥ 0.97) with a slope ratio of 1.0 all the way to 32 steps, whereas at 20 ms the cosine is 0.41 at 8 steps and near zero at 16. For Ant and Go1, explicit and implicit integration at 2 ms are indistinguishable. The Humanoid states visited by its SHAC policy, which are dominated by falls and impacts, give the shortest usable horizon: at 4 steps the cosine is 0.84–0.89 at 2 ms, 0.74 at 5 ms, and 0.52 at 10 ms, and at 8 steps it ranges from −0.05 (10 ms) to 0.65 (explicit Euler, 2 ms). A randomly initialized Humanoid policy near standing shows the same ordering over longer horizons: consistent gradients to about 16 steps at 2 ms (cosine 0.49–0.74 across start states), to about 8 steps at 10 ms, and fewer at 20 ms.

What does not explain the timestep effect

Two plausible mechanisms were tested on the Humanoid at 10 ms with a 16-step horizon. Softening all contacts and joint limits, raising their constraint time constant from 20 ms to 50 or 100 ms so that each constraint response spans several physics steps, did not restore consistency (cosine 0.09 and 0.15). Increasing the solver budget fourfold (400 iterations, 200 line-search iterations), which would help if the implicit-function-theorem adjoint suffered from unconverged solves, did not change the result (cosine −0.08). Pyramidal and elliptic friction cones gave the same gradient quality. One remaining explanation, which these experiments do not confirm, is that contact sets, stick–slip states, and limit activations are fixed once per physics step: with a larger step, each such event changes the discrete dynamics by a larger jump, and the local gradient misses more of the true change.

Consequence for training

The SHAC window \(T\) must stay inside the horizon over which gradients are informative. On Ant at 10 ms, three otherwise identical training runs reached a mean episode speed of 0.08 m/s with \(T = 32\), 0.30 m/s with \(T = 16\), and 0.72 m/s with \(T = 8\) after 200 epochs (the \(T = 32\) run used a 1000-epoch learning-rate schedule instead of 1500). All training in Section 6 therefore uses \(T = 8\), which is informative for Ant and Go1 in every configuration, so that the configurations differ only in the simulator.

A standard remedy from recurrent-network training also helps the gradient here. Capping the norm of each world's state adjoint at three times the batch median before it is passed to the previous step keeps the Humanoid half-batch cosine at 0.65–0.70 for horizons of 32 and 64 steps, whereas the unclipped cosine at 32 steps is 0.03–0.48. The capped gradient is biased: its slope is 2–3 times the finite-difference slope, but it has the same sign. Section 7 reports how this affects training.

6. Training quadrupeds

SHAC learned Ant and Go1 locomotion from scratch in every simulator configuration. Per training epoch, small timesteps learned as well or better, and only small-step policies transferred reliably to the fine reference simulator.

Ant and Go1 were each trained from scratch with SHAC in five simulator configurations: explicit Euler at 2 ms (the integrator of the earlier study) and implicitfast at 2, 5, 10, and 20 ms. Each configuration ran three seeds for 1000 epochs of 256 environments and 8 control steps, that is, 2.05 million control steps or 11.4 hours of simulated time per run. Only the simulator differs between configurations. No run skipped an update because of a non-finite or exploding gradient.

Per epoch, the 2 ms configurations produced the best policies, and explicit and implicit integration at 2 ms gave similar results (Ant: 6.26 ± 0.06 and 6.37 ± 0.10 m/s; Go1: 1.58 ± 0.06 and 1.56 ± 0.08 m/s in the reference simulator). For Ant, the final reference-simulator speed fell from 6.3–6.4 m/s at 2 ms to 5.2 m/s at 5 ms, 2.7 m/s at 10 ms, and 3.5 m/s at 20 ms, which matches the ordering of gradient quality in Section 5. For Go1, whose reward saturates at 1.5 m/s, the 2, 5, and 10 ms policies all reached the target and walked with the same speed, survival, and lateral drift in the reference simulator as in their training simulator.

The 20 ms Go1 policies did not transfer. They moved at 1.55 ± 0.47 m/s in their training simulator but only 0.84 ± 0.55 m/s in the reference simulator, with 2.6 m of lateral drift instead of 0.1 m. In the reference simulator, the seed-0 policy settles into a crouch and barely moves (Figure 7). These policies exploit motion that exists only at the coarse timestep. The Ant policies trained at 10 and 20 ms were slower but did not lose performance in the reference simulator; their survival was even higher there (89–95%) than in the training simulator (60%), which indicates that the coarse simulators made the Ant flip more easily rather than giving it an advantage.

A 10 ms configuration performs one-fifth of the physics steps of a 2 ms configuration per epoch, so for the same wall-clock time it can train for more epochs. 7. Humanoids

With the SHAC reference tasks, no tested configuration learned humanoid balance. The diagnostics point to short informative gradient horizons on falling states and to the inability of local gradients to value contact-mode changes, rather than to the integrator. Section 8 identifies further differences from Isaac Lab's setups (penetrating initial states, height-based termination, and, for H1, self-collision pairs without adjoint support). It shows that a differentiable gait prior makes the retuned H1 task learnable, and that the joint damping of the original SHAC simulator together with a faster target critic lets the MuJoCo Humanoid learn to run without a prior.

Outcome

The MuJoCo Humanoid falls in 0.66–0.82 s when all actuators are idle. SHAC was run for 81 to 741 epochs in seventeen configurations: the reference reward at 2 and 10 ms, with 8, 16, and 32-step windows, 256 or 512 environments, stronger actuators matching the reference Humanoid (1.75× gear), a pure standing task without the velocity term, lower learning rates, a faster target critic (Polyak factor 0.2, critic learning rate \(2 \cdot 10^{-3}\)), reduced reset noise, softer contacts (including a 100 ms contact time constant that approximates the compliant penalty contact of dFlex, the original SHAC simulator), additional uprightness and angular-rate terms, per-world adjoint clipping, and full-episode back-propagation. In every case the mean episode length ended between 0.77 and 1.21 s. The policies slow the fall slightly but never balance. With the velocity term, they converge to a forward dive: the body stiffens and topples forward to collect velocity reward before termination (Figure 8).

Diagnostics

Several explanations were ruled out. The critic is informative: its value estimate has a rank correlation of 0.74–0.99 with the remaining time before a fall. The step adjoint itself is accurate (Section 5), but on the fall-dominated states that the Humanoid policies visit, the policy gradient stays consistent for only about four control steps, fewer than for the quadrupeds. The contact model is not frictionless: foot contacts are three-dimensional with a friction coefficient of 1. Physical feasibility is not in question either: this is the classic humanoid model distributed with MuJoCo, which model-free reinforcement learning routinely trains to stand and walk.

The simulator's gradients do contain balance information over longer horizons. Open-loop trajectory optimization, which gives each of 64 worlds its own 2 s action sequence and back-propagates through all 100 control steps, raised the mean time upright from 0.72 s to 1.38 s and kept 28% of worlds standing for the full 2 s after 120 Adam iterations. Truncating the back-propagation to 16 steps slowed this progress. A shared feedback policy trained with back-propagation through complete 1 s episodes, however, did not improve.

A humanoid cannot stand still by stiffening its joints. For H1, holding the body upright as an inverted pendulum about the ankle would require an ankle stiffness near \(mgl \approx 500\) N·m/rad, where \(m\) is the mass, \(g\) gravitational acceleration, and \(l\) the height of the center of mass. H1's single ankle pitch joint provides at most 40 N·m. No constant joint-target pose stood for more than 1.5 s. Balance therefore requires stepping, that is, lifting a foot and placing it elsewhere. Stepping changes the set of active contacts. The implicit-function-theorem adjoint differentiates the solution for the current contact set, so it cannot represent the benefit of a contact that does not exist yet. The quadrupeds are statically stable on four feet and can improve by pushing harder or more efficiently within their current contact pattern, which the gradient does capture.

Position servos and a gait clock

Modern humanoid controllers usually command joint targets tracked by proportional-derivative servos. H1-PD replaces H1's torque motors with position servos (\(k_p\) = 40–300 N·m/rad, \(k_v\) = 2–6 N·m·s/rad, torque limits equal to the original motor ranges); its idle fall time increases from 0.5 s to 1.3 s. The servo damping is an actuator velocity gain, the case in which only implicitfast integrates it implicitly. A gait clock gives the policy a 0.8 s phase signal, and a reward term pulls the hip and knee pitch toward an alternating stepping pattern, so that stepping is encouraged by a smooth, state-dependent term rather than discovered through the gradient. After 281 epochs with 512 environments at 5 ms, the policy followed the stepping pattern, and the reward per step rose from 2.0 to 3.0. Episodes still lasted only 1.22 s, no longer than those of the idle robot. Section 8 replaces the joint-angle pattern by a prior on foot heights and foot placement, and combines it with Isaac Lab's servo gains, torso-contact termination, and floor-only collisions; with these changes H1 learns to walk.

8. Retuning the environments and the trainer

This section modifies the tasks to follow Isaac Lab's locomotion environments, reduces the trainer's cost, and tests task, trainer, and gradient-estimator changes. A differentiable gait prior with a foot-placement term made the Unitree H1 humanoid learnable with SHAC. With a faster-updating target critic, H1 reached in 600 epochs a training tracking error that the slower critic reached only after 1000–1400 epochs. Reusing the stored action gradients for further policy updates reached a fixed quality threshold in about 40% fewer epochs at the same epoch cost. With stronger prior weights, the Unitree G1 learned to walk with two of three seeds, and with joint damping and the faster target critic, the MuJoCo Humanoid learned to run without any prior. On the Isaac Lab Go1 task, SHAC usually converged to standing still. The analytic adjoint occasionally gives a single world a gradient 10 to more than 100 times the median over worlds; capping each world's gradient made all four seeds walk on its forward-command variant, against none of the four seeds without the cap; with lateral and yaw-rate commands, no seed learned with 512 environments, and one of four learned with 2048 environments and 1600 epochs. With the cap, SHAC learned every tested command set with a single direction of motion but stood whenever the commanded direction varied.

Procedure

The study proceeded in iterations. Hypotheses came from previous results, from Isaac Lab's environment definitions, and from work on policy optimization with simulator gradients, such as SHAC, its adaptive-horizon successor [Georgiev et al. 2024], and reparameterization proximal policy optimization [Zhong et al. 2025]. Each iteration tested one change or a small group of related changes, screened with training runs of 400 to 1600 epochs. Screening runs shared the GPU with up to two other runs, so their wall-clock times are not comparable; costs are instead reported from separate timing runs in which the GPU partition ran nothing else. The hardware is one Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) partition, a quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, with four CPU cores. Policies were evaluated in the reference simulator of Section 2 (implicitfast, 1 ms), over 10 s instead of the 20 s of Section 2, from 256 randomized initial states. All training in this section uses implicitfast. Table 16 at the end of this section lists the iterations.

Environment changes

The velocity-tracking tasks follow Isaac Lab's flat-terrain locomotion environments for Go1, H1, and G1. The policy observes the base linear and angular velocity in the body frame, the direction of gravity in the body frame, the velocity command \(c = (c_x, c_y, c_\omega)\) of forward, lateral, and yaw velocity, the joint positions relative to a default pose \(\bar q\), the joint velocities, and the previous action. The policy output \(\eta\) sets the targets \(q_\text{target} = \bar q + s_a \tanh(\eta)\) of proportional-derivative servos, with action scale \(s_a = 0.5\) rad; the actuator control \(u\) of Section 1 is then \(q_\text{target}\). The rewards use Isaac Lab's terms and weights. Velocity tracking is rewarded by the kernels \(\exp(-\lVert c_{xy} - v_{xy}\rVert^2/\sigma_c^2)\) for the planar base velocity \(v_{xy}\) and \(\exp(-(c_\omega - \omega_z)^2/\sigma_c^2)\) for the yaw rate \(\omega_z\), with \(\sigma_c = 0.5\) (m/s or rad/s). Penalties act on vertical velocity, roll and pitch rates, tilt, torques, joint accelerations, action changes, and joint-limit violations, and a foot air-time term rewards steps. Isaac Lab multiplies every reward by the control interval (0.02 s); the tasks here omit this factor, which scales all rewards by the same constant and leaves their relative weights unchanged. Commands are resampled every 10 s, and episodes last 20 s (1000 control steps).

Four changes to the simulated robots and resets correct problems of the earlier setup that affect the analytic gradient or the initial states. Their individual effects were not isolated:

  • Servo models. Isaac Lab's proportional-derivative drives, which its physics engine evaluates internally (Isaac Lab's implicit actuators), become MuJoCo position servos with force \(\tau = \operatorname{clip}(k_p (q_\text{target} - q) - k_v \dot q, \pm\tau_\text{max})\), where \(\dot q\) is the joint velocity and \(\tau_\text{max}\) the actuator's torque limit. The force limit is set on the actuator, where the adjoint's saturation handling applies, and implicitfast integrates the \(k_v\) term implicitly. H1 uses Isaac Lab's gains and default pose: \(k_p = 150\)–\(200\) N·m/rad with \(k_v = 5\) N·m·s/rad on hips, knees, and torso, and \(k_p = 20\) N·m/rad with \(k_v = 4\) N·m·s/rad on the ankles.
  • Floor-only collisions. The adjoint supports eleven primitive collision pairs. Other pairs, such as capsule–cylinder and cylinder–cylinder, contribute no gradient without a warning. The Menagerie Go1 and H1 models contain 247 and 127 such self-collision pairs. Like Isaac Lab, which disables self-collision for these robots, the retuned tasks let robot geometry collide only with the floor.
  • Termination by torso contact. Isaac Lab ends a humanoid episode only when the torso touches the ground, with a penalty of 200. All humanoid configurations of Section 7 terminated at a torso height of 0.6–0.74 m instead. The retuned H1 and G1 tasks detect torso–floor contact from the simulator's contact list.
  • Physical initial states. The reset used in Section 7, which follows the SHAC reference Humanoid, places 50% of the MuJoCo Humanoid's initial states in floor penetration of up to 14 cm and 23% in self-penetration, which produces large impulses at the start of an episode. Initial states are now drawn from a bank of states checked for penetration in advance, with Isaac Lab's randomization: random heading, base velocities of up to 0.5 m/s and 0.5 rad/s per axis, and, for Go1, joint positions scaled by a factor between 0.5 and 1.5.

A differentiable gait prior

The analytic gradient treats the contact set as fixed within a step, so it cannot value a foot that is about to lift off or touch down. The pull request's own Go1 trajectory-optimization example addresses this with a reference trajectory for the foot heights. The same idea gives reward terms that are differentiable with respect to the joint positions. A gait clock with period \(P = 0.8\) s assigns foot \(i\) at control step \(t\), with control interval \(\Delta t_c = 20\) ms, the phase \(\psi_i = (t\,\Delta t_c/P + \delta_i) \bmod 1\), with offsets \(\delta = (0, 0.5)\) for the two feet. A foot is in stance while \(\psi_i < D\), with duty factor \(D = 0.6\), and otherwise in swing with progress \(\rho_i = (\psi_i - D)/(1 - D) \in [0, 1)\). Let \(p_i\) be the position of a point under the sole of foot \(i\), computed by differentiable forward kinematics from the joint positions, and let \([\cdot]\) equal 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. Three terms are added to the reward:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_\text{gait} = &-w_h \sum_i \big(p_{i,z} - z_0 - z_\text{sw} \sin(\pi \rho_i)\,[\psi_i \ge D]\big)^2 \\ &- w_s \sum_{i:\,\psi_i < D} \Big\lVert \tfrac{\Delta p_{i,xy}}{\Delta t_c} \Big\rVert^2 \\ &- w_r \sum_{i:\,\rho_i \ge 0.5} \big\lVert \tilde p_{i,xy} - \bar p_{i,xy} - \kappa_v v_b - \kappa_e (v_b - c_{xy}) \big\rVert^2 . \end{aligned} \]

The first term asks the foot to follow a swing arc of height \(z_\text{sw} = 0.08\) m above its standing height \(z_0\). The second penalizes the horizontal speed of feet that the clock places in stance, where \(\Delta p\) is the displacement over one control interval. The third is a Raibert-type foot-placement rule for the second half of the swing, after Raibert's hopping robots, which place the foot ahead in proportion to the velocity. Here \(\tilde p_{i,xy}\) is the foot position relative to the base in the heading frame (the frame rotated with the base's yaw), \(\bar p_{i,xy}\) its position in the default pose, \(v_b\) the base's planar velocity in the heading frame, and \(\kappa_v = \kappa_e = 0.1\) s. Placing the foot ahead in proportion to the velocity arrests a fall, and the term in \(v_b - c_{xy}\) shifts the step to accelerate or brake. The weights are \(w_h = 40\), \(w_s = 2\), and \(w_r = 10\) (\(w_h\) and \(w_r\) in m−2, \(w_s\) in s2/m2); the G1 uses stronger weights, described below. The policy observes \(\sin(2\pi t\,\Delta t_c/P)\) and \(\cos(2\pi t\,\Delta t_c/P)\). The prior prescribes when each foot should be in the air and, through the placement rule, where it should land; the gradient decides how the joints achieve this and how the body balances.

Trainer changes

The trainer was rewritten without changing the SHAC update. Three changes reduce its cost:

  • No host synchronization in the rollout. Resets and non-finite states are handled with masked tensor operations, and episode statistics accumulate on the GPU, so the CPU does not wait for the GPU inside an epoch.
  • A captured critic update. The 64 minibatch steps of the critic regression (16 passes over 4 minibatches) are captured as one CUDA graph, which shortens a Go1 epoch from 119 to 98 ms (Table 13).
  • Stored forward steps. The backward pass of Section 2 recomputes every physics step before applying its adjoint. The step adjoint needs only the step's input and its completed forward data, so the trainer now keeps one simulator state buffer per physics step of the window, each with its own captured forward and backward graph, and calls the adjoint directly. On identical inputs, the action gradients of a 4-step window differ from those of the recomputing backward by \(1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}\) for Go1 and \(4.9 \cdot 10^{-6}\) for H1 (relative norm of the difference). Two recomputing runs differ by as much (\(1.1 \cdot 10^{-2}\) and \(7.5 \cdot 10^{-6}\)), because the GPU's contact detection is not deterministic. In a third Go1 comparison, between two stored-forward runs, the relative difference was 13 because one world's gradient spiked; such spikes occur with both backward variants (Trainer and gradient-estimator variants).

Smaller changes followed:

  • the forward kinematics of the gait prior is evaluated once per control step for all feet at once;
  • one reset sample serves both episode resets and the replacement of non-finite states;
  • the target critic is evaluated once per step;
  • the captured critic update uses an optimizer that updates all parameters in one kernel.

Together with the stored forward steps, these changes shorten an epoch of the Section 6 Ant and Go1 configurations 1.75 times at 2 ms, and of the Ant configuration 2.3 times at 10 ms, compared with the trainer of Section 2 (Table 13).

The target critic

SHAC's target critic provides both the bootstrapped value at the end of each window, whose gradient reaches the actor, and the bootstrapped values in the critic's own training targets. It follows the trained critic with the Polyak update \(\phi_\text{target} \leftarrow \alpha\,\phi_\text{target} + (1 - \alpha)\,\phi\) once per epoch. The humanoid setting of the original SHAC implementation (DiffRL, NVlabs/DiffRL), \(\alpha = 0.995\), gives the target a half-life of 138 epochs: after that many updates, the weight of any earlier critic in the target has halved. For the first few hundred epochs the value gradient at the window end therefore comes largely from an early, nearly untrained critic. With \(\alpha = 0.9\), a half-life of 6.6 epochs, H1 learned 1.7–2.3 times as fast per epoch: after 600 epochs the tracking error was 0.14 ± 0.01 m/s over three seeds, against 0.42 m/s with \(\alpha = 0.995\) after 600 epochs and 0.15 ± 0.02 m/s after 1500. In training, the three \(\alpha = 0.995\) seeds needed 993, 1193, and 1395 epochs to reach the training tracking error, averaged over 20 epochs with mean episodes of at least 16 s, that the \(\alpha = 0.9\) seeds reached after 600 (0.16 m/s).

The MuJoCo Humanoid: joint damping and the target critic

Two changes made the torque-controlled MuJoCo Humanoid of Section 7 learn to run without any gait prior. The task is the SHAC reference task of Section 7 (forward-velocity reward, termination at a torso height of 0.74 m) with the physical initial states, floor-only collisions, and the actuator strength of the reference Humanoid (gears scaled by 1.75). The first change sets the damping of every hinge joint to 10 N·m·s/rad, up from 0.2–5 N·m·s/rad in the MuJoCo model. dFlex, the differentiable simulator of the original SHAC work, applies exactly this damping: its joint-limit model adds the torque \(-b\,\dot q\) with \(b = 10\) N·m·s/rad at every joint at all times, not only beyond a limit (dflex/sim.py, function jcalc_tau; limit_kd=1.e+1 in envs/humanoid.py). The second change is the faster target critic (\(\alpha = 0.9\)).

Damping makes the analytic gradient far more consistent. For a random policy at 2 ms, the half-batch cosine of the policy gradient rises from 0.84 / 0.63 / 0.47 / 0.09 to 0.95 / 0.95 / 0.84 / 0.82 at windows of 4 / 8 / 16 / 32 control steps, and at 32 steps the slope ratio of Section 5 falls from 9.6 to 2.6 (Table 10). Table 11 shows that training needs both changes: with either one alone the policy still dives forward and falls within about 1.3 s, and with both it runs (Figure 12). This agrees with Section 7, where a target critic with Polyak factor 0.2 and a higher critic learning rate did not make the Humanoid without added damping stand (Table 7).

Quadruped results

Below, the Isaac Lab Go1 task means Isaac Lab's flat-terrain Go1 reward, commands, and termination on the Isaac-Lab-equivalent Go1 model described under Table 12. Isaac Lab drives the Go1 with a learned actuator network; here it is approximated by position servos with the gains of Isaac Lab's Go2 motor model (\(k_p = 25\) N·m/rad, \(k_v = 0.5\) N·m·s/rad). Its forward-command variant samples only forward velocities of 0–1.5 m/s. On the Isaac Lab Go1 task, SHAC did not learn to follow velocity commands. It learned to stand (95% survival) with a tracking error of 0.77 m/s, close to the 0.75 m/s expected magnitude of the commanded planar velocity. Restricting commands to forward velocities, adding to the Isaac Lab reward a quadratic tracking penalty or a linear term on the base velocity projected onto the command direction, enlarging the action range to 1 rad, raising the servo gain to 100 N·m/rad, and adding the gait prior with a trot pattern (period 0.5 s, diagonal legs in phase) did not help (Table 16). Proximal policy optimization (PPO), a model-free reinforcement-learning baseline that needs no simulator gradients, learned the task with Isaac Lab's settings, including its action scale of 0.25 rad: its tracking error was 0.10 m/s after 300 iterations (29.5 million steps). With SHAC's action scale of 0.5 rad, PPO also stood still. Both PPO runs used unbounded Gaussian actions, whereas SHAC bounds them with tanh.

In these runs, the reward had a larger effect than the robot model on whether a gait appeared. The SHAC reference reward of Section 6, whose forward-velocity term grows linearly up to a cap, trained both the Menagerie Go1 and the Isaac-Lab-equivalent Go1 to walk within 400 epochs, with one seed each: in the reference simulator they moved at 1.74 and 0.76 m/s with 98% and 100% survival. With the Isaac Lab reward, whose exponential tracking kernels saturate far from the commanded velocity, SHAC more often stood still. On the forward-command variant, clipping rare per-world gradient spikes changed this, but with lateral and yaw-rate commands it did not (Trainer and gradient-estimator variants).

Per-world clipping did not make the full Isaac Lab Go1 task learnable with 512 environments. To locate the failure, the command set was varied one dimension at a time (lower part of Table 12). Trainer and gradient-estimator variants

Two changes to the actor update were tested on the H1 gait task. The first reuses the action gradients. SHAC uses each window's simulator gradient for one actor step. Reparameterization proximal policy optimization (RPO) [Zhong et al. 2025] shows that the gradient of the window objective with respect to each sampled action remains usable for further updates of a slightly changed policy. After the SHAC actor step, the trainer keeps these per-sample action gradients and makes four further actor updates without simulator work. Each update rewrites every stored pre-tanh action as the current policy's mean plus its standard deviation times a recovered noise sample, so the action is unchanged but its stored gradient now reaches the current policy's parameters. Each sample is weighted by its importance ratio between the current and the rollout policy (set to zero outside [0.2, 2]), and 0.5 times the Kullback–Leibler divergence \(D_\text{KL}(\pi_\text{rollout} \,\Vert\, \pi_\theta)\) is added as a penalty. With \(\alpha = 0.9\), the three seeds reached the threshold of Table 14 after 181, 169, and 213 epochs, against 388, 315, and 285 for the three seeds without reuse. After 600 epochs the policies give 99.6 ± 0.4% survival and a tracking error of 0.16 ± 0.02 m/s over three seeds in the reference simulator, similar to the policies trained without reuse (Table 8): the reuse speeds up early learning but does not improve the final policies. Combined with a 10 ms timestep and 1024 environments, the reuse reached the threshold after 156 and 178 epochs (0.57 and 0.65 min at 220 ms per epoch), against 311 epochs without reuse (Table 14), with 99.8 ± 0.3% survival and a tracking error of 0.15 ± 0.02 m/s after 600 epochs. The second change, a \(\lambda_a\)-return actor objective, averages the objectives truncated after \(j + 1\) control steps, each bootstrapped with the critic, with weights \((1 - \lambda_a)\lambda_a^{j}\) for \(j = 0, 1, \dots\) and the remaining weight on the full window, instead of bootstrapping only at the window end. With \(\lambda_a = 0.9\), independent of the critic's \(\lambda = 0.95\), it trained worse: 80% survival and a tracking error of 0.54 m/s (Table 14).

A third change addresses rare gradient spikes. The analytic adjoint occasionally gives a single world a gradient 10 to more than 100 times the median over worlds. From one Go1 state (64 environments, 4-step window), 11 of 80 backward passes with the recomputing backward and 19 of 80 with stored forward steps contained such a world, always world 45, world 59, or both. Whether a spike occurs varies between repetitions, because the GPU's contact ordering is not deterministic. The batch gradient is clipped only by its total norm, so one such world can dominate the direction of the actor update. Per-world clipping caps, at every control step, the norm of each world's state adjoint (the gradient of the objective with respect to that world's simulator state) and of its control gradient at 10 times the median over worlds. Unlike the cap of Section 5, which limits only the state adjoint at three times the median and was used in the clipped-adjoint Humanoid variants of Table 7, this cap is looser and also limits the control gradient. In training it scaled down the state adjoint in 0.7–1.0% and the control gradient in 0.3–0.7% of the Go1 world steps, and in 0.3–0.6% and 0.1% of the H1 world steps. On the forward-command variant of the Isaac Lab Go1 task, all four seeds walked with it, against none of the four seeds without it (Table 12; tracking error per seed with clipping: 0.11 / 0.07 / 0.09 / 0.09 m/s). With caps of 3 and 30 times the median instead of 10 (seed 0, one run each), the Go1 also walked (tracking errors 0.14 and 0.08 m/s), although the cap of 30 scaled down only 0.1% of the state adjoints. This is consistent with the rare large spikes, rather than a general reduction of gradient magnitudes, mattering. Either cap alone sufficed for seeds 0 and 1, which both stood without a cap: with only the control gradient capped, both seeds walked, and with only the state adjoint capped, both seeds walked. On the full Isaac Lab Go1 task, with lateral and yaw-rate commands, clipping did not help: neither seed walked after 800 epochs (tracking error 0.77 and 0.81 m/s, survival 100% and 69%). On the H1 gait task it did not help: with \(\alpha = 0.9\) it gave 97.7 ± 1.1% survival and a tracking error of 0.23 ± 0.06 m/s over two seeds after 600 epochs, against 99.3 ± 0.8% survival and a tracking error of 0.14 ± 0.01 m/s over three seeds without clipping (Table 8).

Two further changes to the gradient itself were tested. The first mixes the analytic and the likelihood-ratio gradient with inverse-variance weights, computed per control step and action dimension from their variances over environments. In a short test on the H1 gait task, it put almost all of the weight on the analytic gradient, because the likelihood-ratio estimate is far noisier; it therefore behaves like SHAC. The second softens the contacts only in the backward pass: the forward physics is unchanged, and the backward pass recomputes each step with an impedance that ramps from 0.01 to 0.95 over 2 cm of penetration. The impedance is MuJoCo's per-contact constraint strength between 0 and 1; larger values are stiffer. 9. Limitations

  • Integrator coverage. For all standard models tested, joint damping is the only velocity-dependent smooth force, so implicitfast and Euler with implicit damping are identical. Go1-kv, H1-PD, and the servo-driven robots of Section 8 exercise the additional terms of implicitfast, but only Go1-kv was compared quantitatively against Euler.
  • Stability protocol. Divergence was measured under vigorous random actuation from near-standing states. Trained policies visit a narrower set of states, so the stable timestep for a given policy can be larger; the 20 ms training runs themselves encountered no non-finite states.
  • Accuracy is local. Integration error was measured over one 20 ms control interval. Long-horizon trajectory differences between timesteps are dominated by chaotic divergence and were not reported.
  • Tuning. In Sections 3–7, SHAC hyperparameters follow the reference implementation except for the window length and the environment count. They were not tuned per configuration, and a configuration-specific window, such as \(T = 16\) at 2 ms, might change the ranking in Section 6. Section 8 changes the target critic's Polyak factor for the humanoids and screens a few trainer variants; the remaining hyperparameters were not tuned.
  • Humanoid result. The negative result of Section 7 covers the variants in Table 7, each trained for at most 741 epochs. The H1 walking policies of Section 8 depend on a gait prior that prescribes when each foot is in the air; they do not show that SHAC discovers stepping without such a prior. Only the three-seed rows of Table 8, the last row of Table 11, the several-seed rows of Table 12, the three-seed G1 results (\(\alpha = 0.9\) with the H1 weights and with stronger weights), and the per-world clipping results with two or more seeds (Table 12, H1, and G1) average over several seeds; all other rows of Tables 8, 11, and 12, the other G1 runs, the cap-factor runs, and all rows of Table 14 come from one training seed each. Table 9 and Figures 10–13 show seed-0 policies. Two terms of the prior were ablated with one seed each: without the foot-placement term (\(\alpha = 0.995\)) H1 did not walk, and without the foot-height term (\(\alpha = 0.9\)) it still walked. The stance-slip term was not ablated.
  • Humanoid models. The running MuJoCo Humanoid policies use a joint damping of 10 N·m·s/rad in training and evaluation, which matches the model of the original SHAC work but not MuJoCo's model. The G1 walked only in the tested configuration with prior weights three and two times those used for H1 together with H1's command ranges, which exclude lateral velocity.
  • Task fidelity. The retuned tasks follow Isaac Lab's definitions but omit its observation noise, mass randomization, pushes, and heading controller. The Go1 servo model approximates Isaac Lab's learned actuator network, and SHAC bounds actions with tanh at scale 0.5 rad instead of Isaac Lab's unbounded actions at 0.25 rad for quadrupeds.
  • Gradient-estimator variants. Backward-only contact softening was trained once (H1, one seed). Per-world clipping was screened with few seeds: at 10 times the median on the Isaac-Lab-equivalent Go1 (four seeds with forward commands, two to four per full-task configuration, and one or two per reduced command set) and on H1 (two seeds); caps of 3 and 30 times the median and the separate state and control caps were tested with one or two seeds each. The full-task result (one of four seeds) is not a reliable success rate.
  • Adjoint limitations of the tested commit. The step adjoint does not account for actuator force saturation in the control derivative (corrected in the harness), does not support mesh collisions, tendons, or connect and weld equality constraints, and has no adjoint tests at this commit. Collision pairs outside its eleven supported primitive pairs contribute no gradient without a warning. For the Euler integrator with implicit joint damping, the derivative of the next velocity with respect to the joint positions omits the configuration dependence of the mass matrix; for a two-link pendulum with joint damping 2 and 20 N·m·s/rad, its relative error against finite differences is 56% and 430%. The training runs in this report used implicitfast or explicit Euler, which are not affected.
  • Hardware. All timings come from one MIG 1g.24gb instance, a quarter of an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, with four CPU cores. Kernel-launch overhead is hidden by graph replay, but the relative cost of learner updates versus simulation depends on the machine and the batch size.

10. Conclusion

At commit 357a75d, the MuJoCo Warp step adjoint supports implicit integration, and implicit treatment of damping does allow larger timesteps. It removes the explicit-damping divergence that limited the earlier configuration to about 10 ms for Go1 and 5 ms for the Humanoid. For the standard models, MuJoCo's default Euler integrator already provides this benefit; implicitfast adds accuracy only when damping is part of the actuator, as for position servos with a derivative gain. Implicit integration does not make 20 ms safe for humanoids under vigorous actuation.

Larger timesteps raise simulator throughput almost in proportion to the step up to 10 ms (4.8–4.9 times from 2 to 10 ms with gradients), and less beyond that for contact-rich robots. End to end, a SHAC epoch at 10 ms is only 2.3–2.7 times faster than at 2 ms, because learner costs are fixed. Capturing the forward and backward steps as CUDA graphs is an independent speedup of 2.7–7.8 times over launching kernels from Python.

Larger timesteps degrade what SHAC needs most, the gradient over a rollout. The horizon over which the analytic policy gradient remains consistent shrinks as the timestep grows, from at least 32 control steps (the longest measured) at 2 ms to about 4 at 20 ms for Go1. Per epoch, Ant learned more slowly at large timesteps. Speed gained by training at 20 ms (Go1) or by training longer at 10 ms (Ant) did not survive transfer to a fine-step simulator, because at these timesteps the simulated dynamics differ measurably from the converged ones.

In the simulator configurations of Section 6, SHAC trained Ant and Go1 locomotion from scratch on the analytic adjoint, provided the window stayed within the informative horizon (8 control steps). With the reference tasks of Section 7, it did not learn humanoid balance. The diagnostics of Section 7 pointed to two limitations: on the falling states a learning humanoid visits, the analytic gradient stays informative for only about four control steps, and balance requires stepping, which changes the contact set in a way that a local gradient taken within the current contact set cannot value. Section 8 suggests that both limits can be relaxed: with the joint damping of the original SHAC simulator, the policy gradient of a random Humanoid policy stays consistent over 32 steps (half-batch cosine 0.82 instead of 0.09), and a gait prior can stand in for the value of a new foot contact.

Modifying the tasks to follow Isaac Lab and adding a differentiable gait prior with a foot-placement rule made the Unitree H1 humanoid learnable with SHAC. With the target critic's Polyak factor reduced to 0.9 from 0.995, the humanoid setting of the original SHAC implementation, 600 epochs gave walking policies with 99.3 ± 0.8% survival over 10 s and a mean planar tracking error of 0.14 ± 0.01 m/s over three seeds in the fine-step reference simulator. This is an estimated 2.3 minutes of training with the GPU partition otherwise idle. Reusing the stored action gradients for further policy updates, as in reparameterization proximal policy optimization, reached a fixed quality threshold in about 40% fewer epochs at the same epoch cost, with similar final policies. Without the foot-placement term the prior did not produce walking. A plausible interpretation is that the placement rule supplies the value of a future foot contact, which the analytic gradient cannot represent, while the gradient shapes how the joints produce the steps and how the robot balances.

The same critic change, combined with the always-on joint damping of dFlex, the simulator of the original SHAC work, let the MuJoCo Humanoid of Section 7 learn to run without any prior; damping made its policy gradients consistent over 32 steps, and neither change sufficed alone. The Unitree G1 balanced but did not step with the prior weights used for H1; with stronger foot-height and placement terms, two of three seeds learned to walk, and one followed forward and yaw-rate commands closely.

Keeping each physics step's forward data in memory removes the recomputation in the backward pass and shortens epochs by 1.2–2.6 times, with gradients that match the recomputed ones up to run-to-run variation and rare single-world spikes that occur with both variants. On the Isaac Lab Go1 task, standing still was a strong local optimum: SHAC converged to it for most seeds, and so did PPO with SHAC's action scale of 0.5 rad. PPO with Isaac Lab's action scale of 0.25 rad learned the task, and the complete SHAC reference reward of Section 6, with its capped linear forward-velocity term, trained both Go1 models to walk (one seed each). Rare single-world gradient spikes of the analytic adjoint are one contributing factor on the SHAC side: capping each world's gradient at 10 times the median over worlds made all four seeds walk on the forward-command variant of the Isaac Lab Go1 task, against none of the four seeds without the cap; with lateral and yaw-rate commands, no seed learned with 512 environments, and one of four learned with 2048 environments and 1600 epochs. Varying the command set one dimension at a time shows that SHAC with the cap learns any single direction of motion but stands whenever the commanded direction varies: from standing, it must learn to step and to point its propulsion in the commanded direction at the same time. The cap did not help H1, and backward-only contact softening, although closer to finite differences in a gradient check, made H1 training fail.

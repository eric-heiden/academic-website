Technical report

Whole-body control of Kimodo G1 motions in Newton

Humanoid motion tracking · QP, sampling and Gauss–Newton MPC · SolverMuJoCo

Abstract

This study evaluates reactive whole-body controllers for native Kimodo G1 motion in Newton. Explicit wrist-pose and reference-velocity tasks substantially improve hand tracking and reduce rapid arm motion relative to an earlier foot-focused Gauss–Newton MPC. A finer MuJoCo Warp prediction step and a coordinate-search fallback improve the use of the rollout budget. The complete optimizer, including physical rollouts and the GPU linear solve, executes in one CUDA graph. The shared configuration retains close ordinary foot tracking with small accuracy tradeoffs, while using bounded joint actuation and an unactuated floating base. Backflip tracking and real-time operation remain unresolved. The compact implementation is accompanied by repeated simulations, motion diagnostics, separate visualizations of predicted candidate trajectories, a QP baseline, a Newton inverse-dynamics audit, and reproducible configurations.

1. Hand tracking and motion smoothness

This revision adds explicit hand-pose and reference joint-velocity tracking to the preceding Gauss–Newton whole-body MPC. The immediate baseline is the foot-tracking controller at commit c4787198, which already produced stable ordinary motions and substantially improved foot clearance over the original sampler. Its objective only penalized wrist height below a clearance threshold; wrist position and orientation above that threshold were not explicit tasks. Joint-position error alone also did not penalize rapid velocity corrections.

The comparison uses the same reference CSVs, initial pose and velocity, 500 Hz MuJoCo Warp plant, original actuator limits, flat floor, and reference timing. The optimizer remains GPU finite-difference Gauss–Newton shooting. The revised configuration uses one iteration with a finer prediction step and reuses finite-difference probes as coordinate-search candidates. The revised task weights and actuation choice are specified below. There is no reference slowdown, floating-base actuation, state correction, or filtering of commands, simulated motion, or video.

Wrist error is the RMS Euclidean distance between simulated and reference wrist-body origins, averaged over both hands and the complete run. Joint-velocity error differentiates the recorded hinge-angle error using the median recording interval, then takes its RMS over time and all 29 joints, excluding two boundary samples. Arm oscillation is the RMS high-frequency component of the angle error over the 14 arm joints: a fourth-order Butterworth high-pass filter above 6 Hz is applied forward and backward to the 100 Hz record, with 0.1 s omitted at each boundary. This diagnostic isolates rapid corrections; it is not a learned or validated perceptual score. The filter is used only for analysis.

Foot position is the center of four sole corners; clearance is their minimum height. Swing-height RMS includes frames with reference clearance above 3 cm. Lift recall is the fraction of those frames with simulated clearance above 2 cm. These thresholds are evaluation diagnostics and do not supply a contact schedule to the controller. Smaller errors and higher lift recall indicate closer tracking, but each metric captures only part of motion quality.

Cost and actuation comparisons

Hand pose alone reduces wrist error while leaving faster arm corrections. Velocity tracking alone produces smoother arms but can miss the hand path. Strong uniform velocity weighting suppresses some jumping lifts. Allocating approximately the same rollout-step work to one iteration at 5 ms instead of two at 10 ms improves jumping clearance. Stronger velocity tracking on the arms can then reduce arm corrections without imposing that same weight on the legs. The coordinate fallback reuses the existing finite-difference trajectories; its effect varies across motions.

GPU execution and latency

All optimization, including the MuJoCo Warp rollout batches, residuals, finite-difference Jacobian, matrix solve, line search, and coordinate fallback, executes on the GPU. The inspected root graph contains 37,624 nodes, including 21,022 kernels and 401 device-to-device copies. The inspection finds no host callbacks or host-memory copies. Its fixed-state replay median is 121.3 ms, versus 695.5 ms for eager submission after one warm-up in each mode. Optimized costs vary between executions; this overhead measurement does not establish bitwise equivalence. The complete graph audit includes kernel names, node types, every timing, and every optimized cost.

Full-run optimizer times in Table 3 use CUDA events, omit the first ten updates, and exclude initialization, capture, plant integration, recording, and rendering. The thirteen final evaluations ran sequentially before graph auditing or rendering. The device is an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition MIG 1g.24gb partition, with a four-CPU host quota. A 10 ms control deadline is still missed: synchronous simulation waits for optimization before advancing reference time and does not include the corresponding physical command delay.

3. Research and candidate implementations

Whole-body control coordinates all actuators while respecting the fact that a floating robot cannot directly command its base wrench. Contact with the ground must supply the required support. A quadratic program (QP) chooses accelerations and forces for the current instant. Model-predictive control (MPC) searches over future commands, applies the first command, and replans from the next measured state. Preview is particularly relevant when a controller must generate takeoff momentum before entering flight.

Kimodo (2026) supplies text-conditioned kinematic motion, including native G1 output. Its root and joint trajectories are references, not a guarantee that this G1 model can realize the required acceleration, contact forces, or landing. ProtoMotions’ preparation documentation describes the native G1 CSV representation and supplies the five public example clips used here.

Ideas for hand tracking and smoothness

The official MuJoCo MPC humanoid tracker tracks articulated body positions and velocities, including hands, and includes joint-velocity and control regularization. This motivates treating the hands as explicit tasks. The present controller penalizes joint-velocity error from the reference, rather than encouraging every joint to stop; rapid intended gestures should remain possible.

HumanTracker (2026) emphasizes that pose errors can miss support and contact defects, and evaluates velocity, acceleration, jerk, and a learned preference measure. That motivates reporting temporal diagnostics alongside feet, hand poses, and videos. The present study uses its own simple diagnostics; it neither evaluates on HumanTracker’s benchmark nor computes its learned HumanScore. The numerical results here are not comparisons with the trackers in that paper.

Reference-Free Sampling-Based Model Predictive Control optimizes cubic Hermite position and velocity knots and uses temporal warm starts. It suggests a future route to smoother command parameterization. The current implementation retains four linear position-offset knots to keep the solver small; Hermite interpolation and an extra output filter are not implemented or evaluated in this revision.

4. Model and controller design

Plant, references, and commands

The plant is the 29-actuator Unitree G1 asset supplied by Newton, with 35 generalized velocities and 36 position coordinates. The first six velocities describe the unactuated free base. Total mass is 33.341 kg. The reference format is world position in metres, a scalar-first root quaternion, and 29 hinge angles in radians. Motion is sampled at 30 Hz and interpolated on the quaternion manifold; velocities use centred position differences where available.

Each reference receives one constant vertical translation that places the lowest initial sole point 2 mm above the ground. Initial pose and velocity match that translated reference. This grants a moving start to walking; it is not a stand-to-walk transition. Thereafter only joint forces advance the plant. No root force, pose correction, reference-state substitution, or teleportation is applied.

MPC runs both the plant and predictor on MuJoCo Warp through SolverMuJoCo’s default GPU backend. PD and QP remain explicit CPU baselines. All current runs use native MuJoCo contacts, the implicitfast integrator, a pyramidal friction cone, sliding friction 1.0, and a 2 ms plant step. Robot self-collision is excluded while the imported floor remains active. The torque mode disables imported drives and computes bounded joint torques at 500 Hz through Control.joint_f. Native PD mode uses force-limited drives. Both command zero force on the six base coordinates. The GPU plant stays in MuJoCo coordinates between steps (update_data_interval=0); Newton states are exported for display. This does not model hardware electrical, thermal, sensing, latency, or torque-speed limits.

The original asset has zero reflected rotor inertia. Early high-gain trials produced numerical instability. The final model adds the positive armature values from the published BeyondMimic G1 configuration. Gains use a 10 Hz natural frequency and damping ratio 2, with armature inertia as the inertia scale. The resulting torque limits come from the imported MJCF actuator-force ranges. Every applied command is clipped to those bounds. The reflected inertia and torque bounds are shared by the configurations; the updated gait controller increases PD gains as described below.

Instantaneous whole-body QP

Let \(q\) be the generalized position, \(v\) its tangent-space velocity, and \(a\) the optimized generalized acceleration. Let \(M(q)\) be the mass matrix, \(b(q,v)\) the gravity/Coriolis bias after subtracting passive forces, \(\tau\) the 29 joint torques in N·m, and \(f\) the stacked Cartesian contact forces in N. The selector \(S\) selects actuated coordinates, and the contact Jacobian \(J\) maps generalized velocity to contact-point velocity. The controller enforces

\[M a+b=S^{\mathsf T}\tau+J^{\mathsf T}f.\]

The six base equations are hard equalities and motor torques have hard bounds. Each foot has four candidate sole points. A point is eligible for support when its reference height is below 3.5 cm and its actual height is below 6.5 cm. Ineligible points have zero force. Eligible points have nonnegative normal force and an inscribed square friction cone with coefficient 0.6. The QP’s coefficient is deliberately more conservative than the plant’s 1.0. This comparison therefore tests complete controller configurations and does not isolate preview from contact-model and objective choices. Forces are normalized by body weight to improve numerical conditioning.

The quadratic objective penalizes errors in joint acceleration, root translation and orientation acceleration, swing-foot acceleration, and stance-foot velocity/height stabilization. Stance constraints are soft tasks because several sole points can be redundant. A small regularizer penalizes accelerations and force. This convex program has 59 variables: 35 accelerations and 24 contact-force components. OSQP solves it with a fixed sparse structure and warm starts. Nonconverged or materially infeasible solutions fall back to bounded joint PD; accepted solutions must also satisfy a scaled constraint-violation threshold of 0.005.

The QP does not optimize future foot placement, a takeoff impulse, or future angular momentum. During flight its zero contact forces leave base acceleration constrained by the dynamics. A kinematically attractive root acceleration cannot be imposed by adding fictitious base actuation. This is a central reason to expect difficulties with arbitrary generated motion, even when the per-step QP is solved correctly.

Sampling MPC

The original sampling configuration copies the same G1 dynamics and optimizes four linearly interpolated knots of joint-position offsets over a 0.5 s horizon. Each update evaluates 1,024 parallel candidates in each of two rounds, with 10 ms prediction steps. Leg/waist knot noise is 0.12 rad; arm noise is 0.048 rad. Antithetic perturbations reduce sampling imbalance, and offsets are clipped to ±0.7 rad. The shifted previous plan and zero-offset reference remain candidates. The second round samples at 0.35 times the first-round noise scale around a cost-weighted mean, while retaining the best first-round candidate for comparison.

Let \(u\) denote the optimized joint-position offset in radians, \(q_j^r\) and \(v_j^r\) the reference hinge positions and velocities, and \(q_j,v_j\) their measured values. Diagonal gains \(K_p\) and \(K_d\) have units N·m/rad and N·m·s/rad. With elementwise clipping at the torque limits \(\tau_{\max}\), the applied command is

\[\tau=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(K_p(q_j^r+u-q_j)+K_d(v_j^r-v_j),-\tau_{\max},\tau_{\max}\right).\]

The predictor implements a force-limited native PD actuator; the plant evaluates the same torque formula explicitly at 500 Hz. The coarser predictor is an approximation. At each step the dimensionless objective adds squared root-position error divided by (0.08 m)², quaternion chordal error (at the archived checkpoint) divided by 0.15², mean squared joint error divided by (0.3 rad)², and a coefficient of 0.1 s²/m² times squared root linear-velocity error. A wrist-height penalty uses coefficient 10,000 m⁻² below the smaller of 0.20 m and the reference wrist height. A further penalty is 1,000 times the sum of squared non-foot ground-normal forces (grouped by body in the updated implementation) normalized by 300 N. It discourages support on any non-foot body, including the hip or knee. These are soft costs, not collision or landing guarantees. The total is the mean stage cost plus one terminal stage cost. State targets are evaluated after each prediction step; contact forces correspond to the interval just integrated.

For dimensionless candidate cost \(J_i\), candidate index \(i\), minimum cost \(J_{\min}\), and temperature \(T=0.2\), the intermediate mean uses weights \(w_i=\exp[-(J_i-J_{\min})/T]\). The weights are normalized by their sum; lower-cost candidates receive greater weight. The final command comes from the lowest-cost evaluated candidate, rather than an unevaluated average. This is annealed predictive sampling with weighted refinement; it is not a faithful reproduction or benchmark of DIAL-MPC, MPPI, or iLQR. No contact schedule is supplied. Nonfinite or overflowed predictions are rejected on the GPU. If all candidates fail, the shifted prior plan is retained and a failure is counted. Plant overflow or nonfinite state aborts evaluation. Prediction allocations allow 48 contacts per world in a shared pool and 192 constraint rows per world; 20 solver iterations, 50 line-search iterations, and tolerance 10⁻⁴ avoid oversolving single-precision dynamics. Rejection and peak-allocation counters are included with every run.

Gauss–Newton shooting

The additional MPC solver optimizes the same four knots directly. With 29 joints this gives 116 scalar parameters. One iteration rolls out the current plan and positive/negative perturbations of each parameter: 233 trajectories in parallel. The perturbation is 0.03 rad. These complete trajectories estimate a Jacobian of the weighted tracking residuals with respect to every knot. This is finite-difference single shooting; it does not use an iLQR backward pass or differentiate the contact solver analytically.

Let \(z\in\mathbb R^{116}\) be the knot vector, \(r(z)\) the stacked dimensionless residual vector, and \(A=\partial r/\partial z\) its finite-difference Jacobian. The objective is \(\|r\|^2\). A damped Gauss–Newton direction \(\delta\), in radians, solves

\[\left[A^{\mathsf T}A+\lambda\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\operatorname{diag}(A^{\mathsf T}A)+\mathbf1\right)\right]\delta=-A^{\mathsf T}r.\]

The damping coefficient is \(\lambda=0.1\); the inner diagonal operation extracts the matrix diagonal and the outer operation constructs a diagonal matrix. The vector \(\mathbf1\) contains ones. A tiled GPU Cholesky factorization solves this positive-definite system. The direction is scaled to have maximum absolute component at most 0.2 rad. Eight fresh rollouts evaluate the unchanged plan, zero offsets, and six step lengths from 1 down to 1/32. The least-cost evaluated trajectory supplies the command. A single iteration therefore evaluates 241 trajectories, including the derivative batch. The default uses one iteration, or 241 trajectories per update. It also compares the best finite-difference probe with the line-search result, providing a coordinate-search fallback without additional physical trajectories. Replanning shifts the previous solution forward in time.

The derivative batch reserves 48 contacts per world in a shared pool; the eight line-search worlds reserve 96 per world. Both allow 192 constraint rows per world. Every finite-difference rollout, residual calculation, matrix product, factorization, line-search rollout, and selection runs in one captured CUDA graph. Reference geometry is precomputed once on CPU; the optimizer contains no CPU dynamics, matrix solve, or per-step device readback. The G1 solve pads the 116 parameters to a 128-by-128 tiled system. This implementation supports at most 127 knot parameters; larger robots or richer command bases would need a different factorization arrangement.

Tracking tasks and inner actuation

The gait configuration increases proportional gains by four and derivative gains by two relative to the original sampling checkpoint, keeping the damping ratio and all motor force bounds. It uses a 0.5 s horizon, 5 ms prediction steps, one Gauss–Newton iteration, and 100 Hz replanning. Root position, root rotation, and mean joint error use scales of 0.04 m, 0.1, and 0.15 rad. Rotation residuals use half of the shortest SO(3) logarithm, so their magnitude is half the angular error in radians; unlike a quaternion chordal residual, they retain slope near a half turn.

The two foot tasks track the horizontal center of the sole corners and their minimum height above ground, with coefficients 300 m⁻² horizontally and 1800 m⁻² vertically. The four corners lie at x = −0.05 or 0.12 m, y = ±0.025 m, and z = −0.035 m in the ankle frame. An ankle-origin task remains available for comparison. Foot orientation has coefficient 10 rad⁻² on the half-angle residual; world-frame root angular-velocity error has coefficient 0.2 s²/rad². The wrist-clearance and non-foot force tasks remain soft penalties. Non-foot normal forces are summed by body before squaring, so finite differences track a stable body index when the contact buffer changes. The same residual definitions supply both the derivative calculation and the acceptance cost. No foot contact schedule or swing labels are supplied to the controller.

Explicit torque mode emits bounded torques at 500 Hz. The default native PD mode supplies position targets to force-limited SolverMuJoCo actuators, embedding the target velocity as \((K_d/K_p)v_j^r\) in the position input. The imported actuators are routed through Newton's target interface so there is exactly one actuator per joint. Both modes leave the floating base unactuated. Native PD reduces one source of predictor/plant discretization mismatch, but the coarser prediction step still approximates the 2 ms plant; the evaluation compares both modes rather than assuming equivalence.

Hand pose and reference velocity

The added hand task uses the two wrist-yaw body frames, whose rigid visual hands follow their pose. For hand index \(h\in\{1,2\}\), let \(p_h\) and \(p_h^r\) be actual and reference world positions in metres, and \(R_h\) and \(R_h^r\) their rotation matrices. Let \(e_h=\tfrac12\operatorname{Log}((R_h^r)^{\mathsf T}R_h)\) be the half-angle rotation vector in radians, using the shortest SO(3) rotation-vector logarithm. With actual and reference joint velocities \(v_j,v_j^r\) in rad/s for hinge \(j\), the added stage cost is

\[\begin{aligned}\ell_{\mathrm{hand,vel}}={}&w_p\sum_{h=1}^{2}\|p_h-p_h^r\|^2\\&+w_R\sum_{h=1}^{2}\|e_h\|^2\\&+\sum_{j=1}^{29}w_{v,j}(v_j-v_j^r)^2.\end{aligned}\]

The selected weights are \(w_p=300\;\mathrm{m}^{-2}\), \(w_R=3\;\mathrm{rad}^{-2}\), and \(w_{v,j}=0.02\;\mathrm{s}^2/\mathrm{rad}^2\) for the legs and waist, with \(w_{v,j}=0.1\;\mathrm{s}^2/\mathrm{rad}^2\) for the 14 arm joints. The resulting cost is dimensionless. As with the existing tasks, each squared error appears both in the scalar rollout cost and as a weighted residual for the finite-difference Jacobian. Wrist clearance remains a separate lower-height penalty. Body poses are precomputed from the 30 Hz reference and interpolated during rollout; the quaternion target is normalized before computing its rotation error.

The first trial used uniform \(w_{v,j}=0.1\) for every joint, which reduced rapid corrections but suppressed some jumping lifts. Lowering this weight preserves more freedom for takeoff. Hand pose alone gives good wrist accuracy but leaves faster arm motion, while velocity tracking alone can produce smooth yet inaccurate hand trajectories. The final comparison changes the complete configuration; individual ablations above indicate which effects were observed under matched settings. No temporal filter is inserted after optimization: the physical simulator executes the selected plan directly through the bounded actuator interface.

5. Can Newton inverse dynamics supply the WBC?

At the tested main commit, Newton exposes experimental eval_inverse_dynamics_passive and eval_inverse_dynamics_force. The first computes caller-owned mass-matrix, gravity, and Coriolis arrays for articulated trees; the second assembles generalized force from those terms and a requested acceleration. These are useful building blocks for inverse-dynamics control. They do not solve contact forces or make the six free-base coordinates actuated.

The audit uses 25 G1 poses from the dancing reference in three velocity conditions: all velocities zero, random joint velocities with the base stationary, and random base plus joint velocities. Random tangent velocities use independent normal draws with standard deviation 2 in m/s or rad/s as appropriate, with seed 123. This is a diagnostic stress test, not a distribution of measured robot velocities. Both solvers use the same model parameters.

Coordinate conventions must be converted before comparison. MuJoCo uses root-origin linear velocity in world coordinates and body-frame angular velocity. Newton uses the floating child’s centre-of-mass linear velocity and world-frame angular velocity for this world-parent free joint. Define \(B(q)\) by \(v_N=Bv_M\), with subscripts \(N\) and \(M\) denoting Newton and MuJoCo. Let \(D\) be the diagonal rotor-armature matrix, which the tested Newton mass API excludes. The comparison therefore uses

\[M_M=B^{\mathsf T}M_NB+D,\qquad b_M=B^{\mathsf T}\left(b_N+M_N\dot Bv_M\right).\]

Here \(\dot Bv_M\) is the acceleration term caused by the changing velocity-coordinate map. Omitting either that term, the centre-of-mass shift, quaternion ordering, or armature produces an invalid parity test. The downloadable diagnostic script implements these conversions explicitly.

Inspection of the floating-joint bias conversion at the tested commit shows a spatial-to-classical acceleration correction based on the floating child’s own mass. For this articulated G1, replacing that contribution with the full mass-matrix response reduces the worst bias error in the expanded audit to about 0.0018 in N or N·m. The diagnostic correction is \(b_N\leftarrow b_N-M_N[:,0:3]s+[m_0s;0]\), where \(s=\omega\times v_{N,\mathrm{lin}}\), \(\omega\) is root angular velocity, \(v_{N,\mathrm{lin}}\) is root-body centre-of-mass linear velocity, and \(m_0\) is root-body mass. It is evidence for an articulated acceleration-convention issue, not a general fix validated for every Newton articulation.

The shipped QP consequently obtains its dynamics from SolverMuJoCo’s public mj_model/mj_data interface. No Newton core change or runtime bias patch is included. Newton’s mass and gravity evaluations are promising replacements, and the QP accepts externally supplied mass/bias terms for future integration. A supported replacement should first pass moving-base and multi-link regression tests and clarify armature and acceleration conventions. MPC can proceed independently because it needs forward rollouts rather than an inverse-dynamics API.

7. Limitations and next steps

Explicit wrist-pose and reference-velocity tasks improve the ordinary motions studied here, but do not make tracking exact or establish general humanoid stability. The controller still compromises among hand, root, foot, and joint targets under actuator limits. The clips used for tuning are short; the standing gestures provide additional checks, not a large held-out benchmark. Residual arm oscillation and joint-velocity error are simple diagnostics and do not replace visual or human-preference assessment.

The backflip reference is kinematic. In a diagnostic interval from 1.77 to 2.20 s, both modeled soles are more than 3 cm above the floor. After omitting one frame at each boundary, reference center-of-mass acceleration implies an unexplained force with RMS magnitude 742 N, compared with approximately 327 N body weight. Centroidal angular momentum also varies substantially. The diagnostic and measurements use finite differences of the 30 Hz reference and are sensitive to sampling and reference noise. They argue against exact ballistic feasibility; they do not prove that a nearby backflip cannot be executed. Earlier tuned sampling recovered upright in three trials, with assisted landings in two, as documented in the preceding evaluation. Reliable clean acrobatics remain unresolved.

Computation exceeds the 10 ms deadline on the tested GPU partition. Synchronous simulation waits for every optimization, so playback does not include command delay. Known flat ground, exact state feedback, a matched inertial model, excluded robot self-collision, and reference-consistent initial velocity simplify the problem. No hardware, terrain, sensor-noise, actuator-delay, torque-speed, disturbance, or long-duration looping evaluation is claimed.

Further work could test a smoother joint-command basis, a hybrid sampling/gradient optimizer, and physically feasible reference adjustment for flight and landing. These options should be compared under the same latency budget and physical model. The QP remains an inexpensive standing-gesture baseline; the Newton inverse-dynamics audit identifies a separate floating-base bias issue and is not presented as a production-ready dynamics replacement. Cross-robot generality would require replacing the G1-specific body mapping, actuation values, and reference format.

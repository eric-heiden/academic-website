Technical report

Whole-body control of Kimodo G1 motions in Newton

Humanoid motion tracking · QP and sampling MPC · SolverMuJoCo

Abstract

A motion generator specifies how a robot should move; a whole-body controller must produce forces that make the motion possible. This study compares joint proportional–derivative control, a contact-force quadratic program, and a small sampling model-predictive controller on native Kimodo G1 reference motions. Every measured trajectory is advanced by Newton’s SolverMuJoCo using bounded joint torques and a free floating base. The quadratic program balances standing gestures efficiently but fails on the dynamic clips. Sampling MPC tracks walking, dancing, jumping jacks, and a backflip-prompt sequence substantially better, at a computational cost that exceeds its control deadline. The backflip trial recovers upright but includes a brief hand contact. An independent audit finds close agreement between Newton and MuJoCo mass matrices, while identifying a floating-base bias discrepancy in the current experimental inverse-dynamics API. The result is a compact research example with measured capabilities and explicit limits, rather than a verified real-time or hardware controller.

1. Measured results

The main comparison uses one fixed sampling seed, 123, with the same model, initial state, reference timing, gains, and torque bounds for all controllers. Standing lasts 10 s, the five public Kimodo clips are evaluated for 5 s, and the generated backflip sequence lasts 6 s. References shorter than the test duration hold their last pose with zero target velocity. There is no slowdown, phase adjustment, training, or state reset after initialization.

Root error is the Euclidean distance between simulated and reference pelvis positions, in metres. Joint error averages the squared angular errors over all 29 actuated joints before averaging over time. Root-mean-square error (RMSE) includes the entire run, including motion after a fall; smaller values mean closer tracking. “Upright ending” means final pelvis height above 0.55 m and the pelvis up axis having a vertical component above 0.7. It is a recovery criterion, not proof of uninterrupted balance or a clean landing.

The 500 Hz contact-force log confirms 0.042 s of right-wrist/hand ground contact above 1 N normal force, with 5.94 N·s accumulated normal impulse. This threshold excludes negligible numerical contact. Walking, dancing, jumping jacks, waving, and high five have no non-foot ground contact above that threshold in the measured MPC runs. The backflip case saturates at least one motor on 7.3% of physics steps. No extra actuator strength is supplied for the acrobatic trial.

2. Research and candidate implementations

Whole-body control coordinates all actuators while respecting the fact that a floating robot cannot directly command its base wrench. Contact with the ground must supply the required support. A quadratic program (QP) chooses accelerations and forces for the current instant. Model-predictive control (MPC) searches over future commands, applies the first command, and replans from the next measured state. Preview is particularly relevant when a controller must generate takeoff momentum before entering flight.

Kimodo (2026) supplies text-conditioned kinematic motion, including native G1 output. Its root and joint trajectories are references, not a guarantee that this G1 model can realize the required acceleration, contact forces, or landing. ProtoMotions’ preparation documentation describes the native G1 CSV representation and supplies the five public example clips used here.

The implementation decision is to retain both a transparent QP baseline and a small full-order sampling MPC. The QP answers whether instantaneous contact-force allocation is sufficient. MPC answers whether short-horizon physical prediction can track the same motions without explicit gait or contact labels. The experiments support the second approach for dynamic references, while leaving real-time efficiency unresolved. A reduced-order walking planner plus QP remains attractive if reliable walking becomes the narrower objective.

3. Model and controller design

Plant, references, and commands

The plant is the 29-actuator Unitree G1 asset supplied by Newton, with 35 generalized velocities and 36 position coordinates. The first six velocities describe the unactuated free base. Total mass is 33.341 kg. The reference format is world position in metres, a scalar-first root quaternion, and 29 hinge angles in radians. Motion is sampled at 30 Hz and interpolated on the quaternion manifold; velocities use centred position differences where available.

Each reference receives one constant vertical translation that places the lowest initial sole point 2 mm above the ground. Initial pose and velocity match that translated reference. This grants a moving start to walking; it is not a stand-to-walk transition. Thereafter only joint forces advance the plant. No root force, pose correction, reference-state substitution, or teleportation is applied.

SolverMuJoCo uses its CPU backend, native MuJoCo contacts, the implicitfast integrator, an elliptic friction cone, 50 solver iterations, and a 2 ms physics step. The plant uses sliding friction coefficient 1.0. Self-collision is disabled. Imported joint drives are disabled; a 500 Hz local PD loop and torque clipping are implemented through Newton’s Control.joint_f. The default optimizer updates every 10 ms. This evaluates simulated bounded torque commands, not the complete electrical, thermal, latency, or torque-speed envelope of G1 hardware.

The original asset has zero reflected rotor inertia. Early high-gain trials produced numerical instability. The final model adds the positive armature values from the published BeyondMimic G1 configuration. Gains use a 10 Hz natural frequency and damping ratio 2, with armature inertia as the inertia scale. The resulting torque limits come from the imported MJCF actuator-force ranges. Every applied command is clipped to those bounds. This model change is shared by all final baselines; unstable early trials are excluded from the comparison.

Instantaneous whole-body QP

Let \(q\) be the generalized position, \(v\) its tangent-space velocity, and \(a\) the optimized generalized acceleration. Let \(M(q)\) be the mass matrix, \(b(q,v)\) the gravity/Coriolis bias after subtracting passive forces, \(\tau\) the 29 joint torques in N·m, and \(f\) the stacked Cartesian contact forces in N. The selector \(S\) selects actuated coordinates, and the contact Jacobian \(J\) maps generalized velocity to contact-point velocity. The controller enforces

\[M a+b=S^{\mathsf T}\tau+J^{\mathsf T}f.\]

The six base equations are hard equalities and motor torques have hard bounds. Each foot has four candidate sole points. A point is eligible for support when its reference height is below 3.5 cm and its actual height is below 6.5 cm. Ineligible points have zero force. Eligible points have nonnegative normal force and an inscribed square friction cone with coefficient 0.6. The QP’s coefficient is deliberately more conservative than the plant’s 1.0. This comparison therefore tests complete controller configurations and does not isolate preview from contact-model and objective choices. Forces are normalized by body weight to improve numerical conditioning.

The quadratic objective penalizes errors in joint acceleration, root translation and orientation acceleration, swing-foot acceleration, and stance-foot velocity/height stabilization. Stance constraints are soft tasks because several sole points can be redundant. A small regularizer penalizes accelerations and force. This convex program has 59 variables: 35 accelerations and 24 contact-force components. OSQP solves it with a fixed sparse structure and warm starts. Nonconverged or materially infeasible solutions fall back to bounded joint PD; accepted solutions must also satisfy a scaled constraint-violation threshold of 0.005.

The QP does not optimize future foot placement, a takeoff impulse, or future angular momentum. During flight its zero contact forces leave base acceleration constrained by the dynamics. A kinematically attractive root acceleration cannot be imposed by adding fictitious base actuation. This is a central reason to expect difficulties with arbitrary generated motion, even when the per-step QP is solved correctly.

Sampling MPC

The MPC copies the same G1 MuJoCo model as a forward predictor. It searches over four linearly interpolated knots of joint-position offsets over a 0.5 s horizon, rather than sampling an independent torque at every time step. Each update evaluates 128 candidates in each of two search rounds, at noise scales 1 and 0.35, with 10 ms prediction steps and four rollout threads. Leg/waist knot noise has standard deviation 0.12 rad and arm noise 0.04 rad. The previous plan is shifted forward and included as a candidate; a zero-offset reference candidate is also retained.

Let \(u\) denote the optimized joint-position offset in radians, \(q_j^r\) and \(v_j^r\) the reference hinge positions and velocities, and \(q_j,v_j\) their measured values. Diagonal gains \(K_p\) and \(K_d\) have units N·m/rad and N·m·s/rad. With elementwise clipping at the torque limits \(\tau_{\max}\), the applied command is

\[\tau=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(K_p(q_j^r+u-q_j)+K_d(v_j^r-v_j),-\tau_{\max},\tau_{\max}\right).\]

The predictor represents this as a force-limited PD actuator. The plant computes the same form explicitly at 500 Hz; the coarser predictor integration is an intentional approximation. At each prediction step, the objective combines squared root-position error scaled by 0.1 m, quaternion chordal orientation error scaled by 0.15, mean squared joint error scaled by 0.3 rad, a root-velocity penalty, and a small offset penalty. The total is the mean stage cost plus one terminal stage cost. Returned rollout states are compared with reference states after the corresponding prediction step.

The lowest-cost candidate becomes the next plan. This is best-candidate annealed predictive sampling, not an exact implementation or benchmark of DIAL-MPC, MPPI, or iLQR. Contact is resolved inside the forward model; no contact schedule is supplied. Nonfinite costs and rollouts whose clocks reveal MuJoCo’s automatic reset are rejected. The plant also aborts on a reset or nonfinite state. This prevents a simulator reset from being counted as recovery.

4. Can Newton inverse dynamics supply the WBC?

At the tested main commit, Newton exposes experimental eval_inverse_dynamics_passive and eval_inverse_dynamics_force. The first computes caller-owned mass-matrix, gravity, and Coriolis arrays for articulated trees; the second assembles generalized force from those terms and a requested acceleration. These are useful building blocks for inverse-dynamics control. They do not solve contact forces or make the six free-base coordinates actuated.

The audit uses 25 G1 poses from the dancing reference in three velocity conditions: all velocities zero, random joint velocities with the base stationary, and random base plus joint velocities. Random tangent velocities use independent normal draws with standard deviation 2 in m/s or rad/s as appropriate, with seed 123. This is a diagnostic stress test, not a distribution of measured robot velocities. Both solvers use the same model parameters.

Coordinate conventions must be converted before comparison. MuJoCo uses root-origin linear velocity in world coordinates and body-frame angular velocity. Newton uses the floating child’s centre-of-mass linear velocity and world-frame angular velocity for this world-parent free joint. Define \(B(q)\) by \(v_N=Bv_M\), with subscripts \(N\) and \(M\) denoting Newton and MuJoCo. Let \(D\) be the diagonal rotor-armature matrix, which the tested Newton mass API excludes. The comparison therefore uses

\[M_M=B^{\mathsf T}M_NB+D,\qquad b_M=B^{\mathsf T}\left(b_N+M_N\dot Bv_M\right).\]

Here \(\dot Bv_M\) is the acceleration term caused by the changing velocity-coordinate map. Omitting either that term, the centre-of-mass shift, quaternion ordering, or armature produces an invalid parity test. The downloadable diagnostic script implements these conversions explicitly.

With a moving floating base, the uncorrected bias differs substantially, including actuated joint torques. The discrepancy is not removed by coordinate conversion alone.

Inspection of the floating-joint bias conversion at the tested commit shows a spatial-to-classical acceleration correction based on the floating child’s own mass. For this articulated G1, replacing that contribution with the full mass-matrix response reduces the worst bias error in the expanded audit to about 0.0018 in N or N·m. The diagnostic correction is \(b_N\leftarrow b_N-M_N[:,0:3]s+[m_0s;0]\), where \(s=\omega\times v_{N,\mathrm{lin}}\), \(\omega\) is root angular velocity, \(v_{N,\mathrm{lin}}\) is root-body centre-of-mass linear velocity, and \(m_0\) is root-body mass. It is evidence for an articulated acceleration-convention issue, not a general fix validated for every Newton articulation.

The shipped QP consequently obtains its dynamics from SolverMuJoCo’s public mj_model/mj_data interface. No Newton core change or runtime bias patch is included. Newton’s mass and gravity evaluations are promising replacements, and the QP accepts externally supplied mass/bias terms for future integration. A supported replacement should first pass moving-base and multi-link regression tests and clarify armature and acceleration conventions. MPC can proceed independently because it needs forward rollouts rather than an inverse-dynamics API.

5. Reproduction and artifacts

The code starts from Newton main 31f58571 and is published on the G1 WBC branch (commit 6daa44a3). The implementation comprises the example wrapper, reference and QP module, and sampling MPC module. OSQP is an optional dependency under the wbc extra, with an Apache-2.0 license; no new core dependency is required.

git clone --branch feature/g1-kimodo-wbc https://github.com/eric-heiden/newton.git
cd newton
uv sync --extra wbc
uv run --extra wbc -m newton.examples robot_g1_wbc --viewer null --test --num-frames 500
curl -L https://reports.eric-heiden.com/g1-whole-body-control/assets/motions/walk.csv -o walk.csv
uv run --extra wbc -m newton.examples robot_g1_wbc --viewer null --motion walk.csv --controller mpc --num-frames 250 --output walk_mpc

The main command-line controls are --controller pd|qp|mpc, --mpc-samples, --mpc-rounds, --prediction-dt, --control-rate, --seed, and --slowdown. Removing --viewer null opens the Newton viewer. A terminal-held reference is used after a clip ends; looping a locomotion clip would require position and phase stitching that this example does not implement.

Provenance and SHA-256 hashes identify the source URLs, downloaded motion files, generation settings, software versions, and environment. The five public clips are pinned to ProtoMotions commit 607ca7a0bb92e261120bcab8d9f97f28b3130ffc. The sixth is a 6 s output from the public Kimodo-G1-RP-v1 demo using seed 42 and 100 generation steps. Its text requests a standing backward flip and recovery; the resulting sequence includes a turn and is evaluated as generated. The saved CSV, rather than future service output, is the reproducible reference.

The sequential evaluator runs the complete comparison. Unrounded results, plot data, inverse-dynamics measurements, and per-run NPZ trajectories linked from the results table are available. NPZ rows columns are time (s), pelvis height (m), root position error (m), root rotation error (rad), joint RMSE (rad), optimizer time (ms), peak torque-limit ratio, and scaled QP residual. qpos and reference use the scalar-first MuJoCo convention. Where present, contacts records time, total ground-normal force (N), and non-foot ground-normal force (N) at 500 Hz.

Validation includes quaternion sign/interpolation and endpoint tests, QP base-force balance and torque/contact constraints, seeded MPC determinism, and the 10 s standing example in test mode. The complete repository pre-commit checks pass. The final trajectories are finite, respect the applied torque bounds, and contain no simulator reset. These checks establish implementation consistency; they do not certify stability for unseen motions.

6. Limitations and next steps

The tested sampling MPC is the most useful compact controller here for diverse reference motion. It handles changing contact and flight without a separate contact planner. The QP is preferable for inexpensive standing balance and slowly changing gestures, but a stand-alone instantaneous QP is not sufficient for the tested dynamic references. Neither result is a formal stability guarantee.

The strongest limitation is computation: synchronous simulation removes the action delay that a real-time implementation would experience. Exact simulated state feedback, known flat ground, a matched model, disabled self-collision, and reference-consistent initial velocity also simplify the problem. No hardware, sensor noise, actuator latency, disturbance rejection, terrain variation, or long-duration looping evaluation is included. A single main seed and a small set of clips cannot establish broad reliability. The backflip’s hand contact and the joint tracking errors remain material limitations even when the robot ends upright.

The next implementation priority is to reduce prediction cost while preserving landing quality: test a converged batched MuJoCo Warp predictor, improve the spline search space, or integrate MuJoCo iLQR. Any faster variant should be evaluated with measured command delay and multiple seeds, and should include a landing objective that discourages unintended hand contact. If the target narrows to robust walking, a reduced-order foot-placement planner feeding the QP is a more focused alternative.

The controller interfaces leave a reasonable path to quadrupeds: replace the asset, reference mapping, actuator parameters, and support-point list, then retune physical cost scales. The present implementation and measurements are specific to G1 with a free root and scalar hinge actuators. Cross-robot generality has not been evaluated.

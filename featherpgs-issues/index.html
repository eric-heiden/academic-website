FeatherPGS grasp stability

A Franka arm picks up a 40 mm cube and shakes it. The previous version of this page concluded that stable grasping needed fixes across contact construction, friction, articulation and GPU execution. Re-running everything on current code shows that one missing feature explains almost all of it: FeatherPGS never removes gravity from the arm's position drive, so a controller tuned against SolverMuJoCo's gravity compensation can never reach the pose the task waits for. Supply that one torque and the same solver holds the cube to 0.82 mm at the original gains — and to 0.32 mm with more solver iterations, matching SolverMuJoCo. What is left is a shorter and much more specific list — and the two-pad coupon that produced the original report's most alarming number turns out to have been measuring a quantity that does not exist under a hard contact model.

Summary

The grasp was never the problem. The arm could not get into position.

The test is the same scene the original bug report used — StoneT2000/newton-tests, franka_cube_shake.py, unmodified: a fixed FR3 arm, analytic IK, a scripted approach→descend→grasp→lift→shake sequence, 60 Hz control, 16 physics substeps. The task only advances a phase once the end effector is within 5 mm and 2° of its target. Its SolverMuJoCo settings come from the earlier grasp-shake-drift investigation and are left exactly as they are; the only thing changed here is the solver.

What actually happens

The scene gives every arm joint full gravity compensation through two MuJoCo-specific attributes, mujoco:jnt_actgravcomp and mujoco:gravcomp. Those attributes are read by exactly one solver. Under FeatherPGS the position drive has to hold the arm's own weight with proportional gain alone, so every joint settles at tau_gravity / ke below its target — 4.65° on the shoulder lift joint and 2.16° on the elbow. Under SolverMuJoCo the same joints sit at zero error. The pose gate never opens, the task never leaves its first phase, and the grasp is never even attempted.

Raising the gains 100× shrinks the sag to 0.046° and lets the task run, which is what the earlier report did. But the same multiplier lands on the two finger joints, so the grip becomes 100× stiffer than intended. The cube is lifted, works its way out of the pads over the next few seconds, and is dropped 3.65 s into the shake. At 30× it is dropped within a tenth of a second. The workaround replaced a control problem with a contact problem, and only looked like a fix because the run was short enough to end first.

What fixes it

Feed the arm the generalized gravity torque and leave every gain alone. The cube then stays within 0.82 mm of where it started for the whole shake, and within 0.32 mm if the solver runs 32 iterations instead of 12.

FeatherPGS already computes this quantity. Its inverse-dynamics pass builds joint_tau with gravity in it; a per-joint flag that subtracts the gravity contribution is the whole feature. Everything else in the drive is already correct and already implicit.

Scope. One machine, one GPU, one scene, one environment per run. The speed numbers in particular are single-environment numbers and say nothing about FeatherPGS at thousands of parallel worlds, which is what it is built for.

The blocker

The position drive has no gravity term, so the arm sits below its target forever.

FeatherPGS's drive is not a naive spring. It is folded into the mass matrix, which makes it unconditionally stable: you can raise ke as far as you like without the simulation exploding. That is exactly why the 100× workaround appeared to work, and exactly why it hid the real problem.

Solving (H + diag(armature) + diag(K)) qdd = tau_rigid + u0 is the implicit backward-Euler PD. It is correct. What it does not contain is any term that cancels gravity, and nothing else in the solver supplies one: searching the whole package for gravcomp, actgravcomp, or any gravity-cancelling bias returns nothing. The only matches in Newton are inside the MuJoCo solver.

So the steady state of every joint is q = q_target − tau_gravity / ke. Holding the start pose for two seconds and measuring how far each joint sinks makes the relationship exact:

4.65° of joint error puts the end effector centimetres from where the IK asked for it. The task's phase gate wants 5 mm and 2°, so it never opens. Scaling the gains does eventually get the arm there, but the same multiplier lands on the two finger joints, and the grasp degrades as fast as the tracking improves:

The workaround is worse than it looks. The gain multiplier is applied to joint_target_ke[:9], which includes both finger joints. The gripper target closes well past the cube surface on purpose, so squeeze force is proportional to the finger gain; a 100× gain is roughly 100× the intended squeeze. The pads sink 3.86 mm into the cube at 100× against 0.045 mm for the corrected run, and the cube is squeezed out rather than held: the 100× run has no finger-to-cube contact at all in 265 of its 488 shake frames.

Supplying the missing torque

For the measurements on this page the gravity torque is computed outside the solver and written into control.joint_f, so nothing about the solver or the scene's gains is changed. The gradient is taken numerically only so the result cannot be accused of borrowing the solver's own internals.

scripts/gravcomp.py
def _potential(self, q_np):
    """Gravitational potential energy of the arm links at joint configuration q."""
    self._q.assign(q_np.astype(np.float32))
    newton.eval_fk(self.model, self._q, self.qd_zero, self.scratch)
    bq = self.scratch.body_q.numpy()
    u = 0.0
    for b in np.nonzero(self.mask)[0]:
        world_com = transform_point(bq[b], self.com[b])
        u -= self.mass[b] * float(np.dot(self.g, world_com))
    return u

def torque(self):
    """tau_g = dU/dq, the generalized force the drive has to cancel."""
    q = self.env.state_0.joint_q.numpy().copy()
    base = self._potential(q)
    tau = np.zeros(self.n, dtype=np.float32)
    for i in range(self.n):
        qp = q.copy()
        qp[i] += self.eps
        tau[i] = (self._potential(qp) - base) / self.eps
    return tau

def apply(self):
    f = self.env.control.joint_f.numpy()
    f[: self.n] = self.torque()
    self.env.control.joint_f.assign(f)
Where this belongs. Stage 1 of step() already runs a recursive Newton–Euler pass that produces joint_tau including the gravity bias. A per-DOF opt-in that runs the same pass with gravity = 0 and subtracts the difference is the entire feature, and it is the shape MuJoCo already exposes through actgravcomp. Doing it inside the solver also makes it free — the external version above costs eight extra forward-kinematics evaluations and a device sync per frame.
Grasp quality

With the arm in position, FeatherPGS holds the cube about as well as SolverMuJoCo.

The measurement is the cube's position expressed in the gripper's own frame, referenced to the moment the shake starts. That removes the arm's commanded motion and leaves only relative movement between cube and hand — slip, squeeze, and rattle.

Over the full 900-frame run the cube stays within 0.82 mm of where it started. Extending the same configuration to 2000 frames — just under thirty seconds of continuous shaking — gives 1.16 mm, so the residual creep is not a slow leak that eventually loses the object.

Which solver options actually help

With the controller no longer the bottleneck, the solver options can be judged on their own. Two of them help substantially — more iterations, and a better friction formulation — one is neutral, and one is actively harmful.

How much of this needs tuning

The premise of a hard contact model is that you should not have to tune contact parameters to get a grasp. That holds here: the grasp survives every value of the position-correction factor from zero to 0.9 and every value of the regularisation term across four orders of magnitude, and it survives dropping the cube's friction coefficient to a quarter of its authored value. What the sweeps also show is that there is not much point tuning them either.

The regularisation term does almost nothing across four orders of magnitude, which matches what the code does with it: it is added to the Gauss-Seidel denominator but not to the residual the sweep drives to zero, so it changes how fast the sweep converges, not what it converges to. If you were expecting it to soften the contact, it will not.

The position-correction factor is different. The grasp survives every value from zero to 0.9, so it is not something you have to get right to make the task work — but the residual creep bounces between 0.33 mm and 4.50 mm with no pattern. Combined with the non-monotone iteration behaviour, that is the honest characterisation: with an under-converged sweep the creep magnitude is not a smooth function of the parameters, so there is nothing useful to tune. The way to a smaller number is to converge harder, not to search.

The friction sweep shows the combination rule from the other side. Dropping the cube from 1.2 to 0.2 quadruples FeatherPGS's creep, from 0.72 mm to 2.82 mm, and leaves SolverMuJoCo completely unmoved at 0.21 mm. Part of that is the rule: MuJoCo takes the maximum of the two coefficients, so the 1.0 finger pads dominate and the edit is invisible to it, while FeatherPGS averages and the effective coefficient falls from 1.1 to 0.6. The rest is that FeatherPGS's grasp is friction-limited in this range and MuJoCo's is not. Both hold the cube at every value tested, so this is about margin rather than failure — but a material change that one solver ignores and the other responds to fourfold is the kind of thing that makes a model feel untrustworthy.

What to use today

Nothing on this page required tuning a contact parameter. The only settings that had to be chosen were structural — a solve mode that can hold the scene's constraint rows, a row budget large enough to hold them, and an execution flag that lets the frame be captured. Friction, compliance, Baumgarte and the regularisation term were all left at their defaults.

Minimal reproduction

Two pads and one object, with the arm removed entirely.

Stripping the scene down to two pads and one object is the right way to look at creep and slip: no IK, no gravity compensation, no seven-joint arm. But how the pads are held matters more than it looks, and the coupon the earlier report used was measuring something that has no answer.

Static pads

The pads are welded to the world and the object is placed between them with a fixed geometric overlap, then shaken by a body force. This is the shape of the earlier report's minimal reproduction. It has one attractive property — nothing to tune but the overlap — and one fatal one, below.

Driven pads

The pads are the fingers of a four-joint carrier that closes on the object, lifts it off a support, and shakes it, all position driven. Grip force comes from the finger drive rather than from geometry. Four joints, four links, one object, and a fixed support to start from.

The static coupon has no grip force to find

Under a hard contact model, two immovable pads and a fixed overlap do not define a squeeze. Push the object equally hard from both sides and it does not move, whether you push with one newton or a thousand. Nothing in the object's motion distinguishes them, so nothing in the solve pins the pair of forces down: both contacts only say "do not penetrate", and neither has a compliance that would let a penetration depth stand in for a force. A compliant contact model does not have this problem, which is why SolverMuJoCo is untroubled by the same scene.

What FeatherPGS produces instead comes from the position-correction term, which asks for the penetration to be removed within one step — from both sides at once, in a sweep that visits one row and then the other. The result is an object that gains energy in proportion to how hard it is nominally squeezed.

The scaling gives the mechanism away. Doubling the overlap from 1 mm to 2 mm doubles the distance the object is thrown, and doubles the sideways component with it. That is the position-correction term. It targets a separation velocity proportional to beta · penetration / dt, so it grows with the overlap and, at a one-millisecond step, a millimetre of squeeze asks for a fifth of a metre per second — applied on both sides, every substep. The next section pins this down directly.

Where the energy comes from

Turning the position-correction factor down settles it completely. At the same 1 mm overlap:

At pgs_beta = 0 the object does not move at all — the drift is exactly zero, not small. Every millimetre the object travels in this coupon comes from the position-correction term, and it grows far faster than linearly in that term because the push feeds back into the next step's penetration. The two opposed rows would cancel if they were solved together; they are not, because the Gauss-Seidel sweep visits one and then the other.

The usual remedy for this is a compliance term on the contact, which turns the penetration back into a force. FeatherPGS has one — dense_contact_compliance — but it does not reach here. Sweeping it from zero to 1e-4 over the ejecting coupon produces bit-identical results, because the contacts between a free object and world-fixed pads land on the free-body row family, whose diagonal is seeded with pgs_cfm and nothing else. A gripper's contacts land on a different family that does have the term.

This is what the earlier report was measuring. Its minimal reproduction used exactly this geometry with a 0.5 mm overlap, and reported the meshed cube "losing contact and falling 44.7 m". A 44.7 m fall is what free fall looks like after about three seconds — the number is the drop, not the defect. Whether a given run survives at all depends on which side of the ejection threshold it lands, and the mesh and the box need not land on the same side — here both survive at 0.5 mm and both are ejected at 1 mm. Read as a mesh-versus-primitive comparison the original was never measuring geometry.

The driven coupon is well posed, and both geometries hold

Replace the welded pads with a real finger drive and the grip force becomes a defined quantity: stiffness times how far past the surface the finger is commanded. Nothing else about the contact model changes.

Commanded squeezeFeatherPGS, boxFeatherPGS, meshSolverMuJoCo, boxSolverMuJoCo, mesh

Each cell is downward slip / sideways lag in millimetres. Downward slip is the object sliding through the grasp; sideways lag is it trailing the shake, which is inertia rather than slipping.

From one millimetre of squeeze upward, FeatherPGS holds the object to 0.23 mm of sideways lag and essentially zero downward slip, and it does not care whether the object is a primitive box or a triangle mesh. SolverMuJoCo on the same coupon needs four millimetres of squeeze to get close, and at one millimetre it lets the object slide 21 mm. That ordering is the right way round: holding an object firmly with a modest squeeze is exactly what a hard contact model is for, and here it delivers.

One caveat on the comparison. The SolverMuJoCo side of this coupon runs the coupon's own defaults, not the tuned contact settings the full arm scene uses. The full-scene comparison earlier on this page is the fair one. What this shows is how much of MuJoCo's grasp quality comes from that tuning — and how little FeatherPGS needs.

The coupon converges much faster than the scene it stands in for. Eight iterations already puts downward slip at the noise floor here, while the full arm scene — sixty to seventy contacts instead of eight — is still improving out past twenty. That is the one thing to be careful about when reasoning from a minimal reproduction: it will tell you what is wrong, but it will not tell you how much solver budget the real scene needs.

The time step is where the coupon says something the full scene could not. On this grasp FeatherPGS still holds the object at a 16.7 ms step — one physics step per control frame — with 1.6 mm of slip, while SolverMuJoCo loses it beyond about 2 ms. That is the hard contact model doing exactly what it is advertised to do. Strip those away and the contact model's own headroom is much larger than the scene suggested.

What the coupon is good for. Built the second way it is a genuinely useful instrument — it isolates contact and friction from the controller, runs in seconds, and reproduces the behaviour of the full scene. Built the first way it produces dramatic numbers that say nothing about the solver's grasping behaviour. If you want a minimal grasp reproduction, drive the pads.
Time step and speed

FeatherPGS tolerates large time steps as well as SolverMuJoCo, and costs about three times as much per step.

The control loop stays at 60 Hz; only the number of physics substeps changes. Accuracy and speed are measured separately, and speed is measured with the physics loop inside a captured CUDA graph so it reflects the solver rather than Python.

Time stepFeatherPGS creepSolverMuJoCo creepFeatherPGS per frameSolverMuJoCo per frame

The per-substep cost being flat across a 16× change in substep count is the whole story: at one environment this is a latency-bound workload, not a compute-bound one. A single 9-DOF arm produces tens of kernel launches per substep, almost all with grids of one to thirty-two threads. The Cholesky factorisation of the 9×9 mass matrix runs on one thread — small_dof_threshold=12 selects the serial kernel below thirteen degrees of freedom — and the whole Gauss-Seidel solve is launched as one tile of thirty-two threads per world, in both the split and matrix-free modes. There is nothing wrong with that at scale; it is simply the wrong shape for one arm.

Three costs are avoidable at any scale:

  • Three debug copies run on every step in every configuration. They are not behind a flag, and nothing in the repository ever reads the buffers they write.
  • Twenty-one separate zero-fills alongside twenty-four kernel launches in the contact-row build stage, ten of the fills only mattering in a mode that is off by default.
  • Thirteen scoped-timer objects per step, plus the generator chain of the parallel-stream dispatch, which short-circuits to nothing when there is only one articulation size but is still constructed at every call site.

Where the time is best spent

There are three ways to spend more time on the same second of simulation: smaller substeps, more solver iterations per substep, or — on an unmerged branch — frozen inner substeps. Put on the same axes, two of them land on the same frontier and the third does not.

Solver iterationsGraspPer frameSpeed

Iterations are the cleanest lever: going from the default twelve to twenty cuts peak creep from 0.72 mm to 0.31 mm, and past thirty-two there is nothing left to buy. (This sweep uses a shorter run than the headline figures, so its numbers sit slightly below them; the comparison within the table is like for like.) The one wrinkle is that convergence is not monotone — eight iterations is worse than four and much worse than twelve. Under-converged Gauss-Seidel does not simply give a slightly softer grasp; it gives a different one, and where a particular iteration count lands is not something you can reason about from the outside. That is an argument for running enough iterations to be past the noisy region rather than for tuning the number.

The unmerged lever, and why it does not help here

An unmerged FeatherPGS branch adds pgs_inner_substeps: several small position substeps inside one solver step, reusing the mass-matrix factorisation and the contact Jacobians instead of rebuilding them. That attacks exactly the cost that dominates here, so it looks like the obvious route to a big step that is still accurate. On this scene it is not.

Solver × inner substepsEffective time stepGraspPer frameSpeed

Two solver substeps with no inner loop gives 3.43 mm at 1.95 ms. Adding four frozen inner substeps — a four-times-smaller integration step — gives 3.51 mm at 5.82 ms. Refining the integration changed nothing, because the error is not in the integration. It is in the contact basis, which the frozen loop deliberately does not refresh. At the same effective step size, plain substepping gives 1.30 mm.

The one place it does earn its keep is rescuing a step that is too large to work at all: at one solver substep the plain solve drops the cube, and four frozen inner substeps turn that into a 7.47 mm hold at 5.9× realtime. But the behaviour is not monotone — eight inner substeps loses the grasp entirely and sixteen drops the cube again — which is what you would expect when positions are advanced far from where the Jacobians were built. As a speed lever for grasping it needs the contact basis refreshed on some cadence, not frozen for the whole step.

Constraint rows

The scene needs 201 constraint rows. The default is 32, and going over is silent.

Every contact between the arm and anything else becomes one normal row plus two friction rows in a fixed-size per-world buffer. Drive rows and joint-limit rows are allocated from the same buffer first, so contacts are the first thing sacrificed when it fills.

Turning on the solver's own high-water telemetry over a full grasp-and-shake gives the real requirement: 201 dense rows at peak, from up to 71 simultaneous contacts. The constructor default is dense_max_constraints=32, which is ten contacts. The gap is not a matter of degree — below 96 rows the gripper cannot pick the cube up at all.

What overflow does

Slots are claimed with an atomic counter. A contact that does not fit rolls the counter back and marks itself dead:

newton/_src/solvers/feather_pgs/kernels.py:2863
    else:
        # Dense path
        slot = wp.atomic_add(world_slot_counter, world, slots_needed)

Three consequences follow from those seven lines:

  • The dropped contact contributes nothing at all — no normal row, no friction, no force. A finger simply stops touching the cube for that step.
  • There is no warning. The entire 15,760-line solver contains no warnings.warn and no logging. The only way to find out is to opt into row_watermark=True and read constraint_row_watermarks() yourself.
  • Which contacts survive is decided by an atomic race. Nothing orders them by penetration depth or by pair. The rollback also means a later single-slot contact can succeed after an earlier three-slot contact failed. Repeated runs on this machine were bit-identical, so the race is stable in practice here — but nothing in the code makes that a guarantee, and no contact keeps a stable row index between steps, which is why the dense path has no identity-matched warm start.

And the row budget is expensive to raise on the default solve path

The failure arrives as a CUDA module-load error inside the first step. And even where it does fit, a quadratic tile is the wrong shape: at 201 rows the solve is 84 KiB of shared memory driven by a single warp, and the whole run drops to about a third of the speed of the matrix-free path.

The escape hatch. pgs_mode="matrix_free" uses a different generated kernel with an O(M) footprint and does build at 256 rows — which is why every measurement on this page uses it. That makes matrix-free mode effectively mandatory for any articulated scene with a real contact set, even though the constructor default is "split". Either the default should change, or the tiled path should move its staging to dynamic shared memory.

The same scene on the constructor's own defaults

Every other measurement here uses the configuration the earlier report settled on. This is what the library gives you if you construct SolverFeatherPGS(model) and change nothing, with the gravity torque supplied so the task can still run:

ConfigurationModeRowsPeak creepSolve speed

Two things fall out of this. On its own defaults the solver never picks the cube up, entirely because of the 32-row budget, and it does so quietly — the arm runs the full scripted sequence over an object that never moves. And raising the split path's budget until it fits the scene costs about three times the wall-clock time, because a 201-row tile is 84 KiB of shared memory driven by a single warp. Matrix-free mode is not an optimisation here; it is the only configuration that is both correct and fast.

Velocity pass

Extra velocity iterations make the grasp worse, because speculative contacts become hard walls.

pgs_velocity_iterations is the PhysX-style split: solve positions with a Baumgarte bias, then clean up velocities without one. It should improve a grasp. It does the opposite, and the reason is one hard-coded argument.

A contact whose surfaces are still apart has a positive gap phi. The correct velocity-level constraint for it is "you may close at up to phi/dt", which is what the position pass builds. The velocity pass rebuilds every right-hand side with the speculative scale forced to zero:

newton/_src/solvers/feather_pgs/solver_feather_pgs.py:4779
self._stage4_compute_rhs_world(
    dt,
    bias_scale=0.0,
    contact_speculative_scale=0.0,          # <-- not a parameter
    joint_limit_speculative_scale=1.0,
    output=self.rhs_unbiased,
)
if self._has_free_rigid_bodies:
    self._compute_mf_rhs_bias(dt, bias_scale=0.0, speculative_scale=0.0,
                              output=self.mf_rhs_unbiased)

Dropping the Baumgarte bias is correct and is the point of the pass. Dropping the speculative term is not: the constraint becomes "you may not approach at all", applied to pairs that are millimetres apart. Every one of them turns into an invisible wall for the duration of the velocity iterations.

The scene's collision gap is 5 mm per shape, so during the shake there are typically 14 touching contacts and 32 speculative ones. Shrinking the gap by 50× so that almost no speculative contacts exist removes the regression and lets the velocity pass do what it is supposed to:

ConfigurationSpeculative contacts livePeak creep
The fix is one argument. The velocity pass should keep contact_speculative_scale=1.0, matching what the position pass already does and what the constraint actually means. As written, the option cannot be used on any scene with a non-trivial collision gap — which is the default configuration of every Newton scene.
Execution

The default execution settings cannot be captured into a CUDA graph.

Every speed number on this page depends on capturing the 16-substep physics loop once and replaying it. With FeatherPGS's constructor defaults, that capture fails.

use_parallel_streamsdouble_bufferCapture of a 16-substep frame

The earlier report blamed both options. Only double_buffer is responsible, and the reason is structural rather than incidental. The double-buffer memset forks onto a private stream and never joins back within the same step:

newton/_src/solvers/feather_pgs/solver_feather_pgs.py:5530
if self._memset_stream is not None:
    with wp.ScopedTimer("DB_Memset", ...):
        with wp.ScopedStream(self._memset_stream):        # forks the capture
            for size in self.size_groups:
                if self._mass_update_global_flag:
                    self._H_bufs[self._buf_idx][size].zero_()
                self._J_bufs[self._buf_idx][size].zero_()
        self._memset_done_event[self._buf_idx] = self._memset_stream.record_event()
        self._buf_idx = 1 - self._buf_idx

wp.ScopedStream synchronises on entry but not on exit. The only join is the wait_event at the top of the next step, so the final step of any captured region leaves an unjoined fork and capture fails. On the first captured step the event being waited on was also recorded outside the capture, which is separately illegal.

There is a method that would fix the second half of this, seed_double_buffer_events(). It has zero callers anywhere in the repository, and its docstring says it must be called inside graph capture, which is the opposite of what would work. No test in the repository captures a FeatherPGS step(), which is why this survived.

Practical effect. Pass double_buffer=False. On a 9-DOF arm the buffers it double-buffers are about 1.5 KB, so the memset it is hiding costs nothing; the extra stream, the per-step event allocation and the extra dependency edges cost strictly more. A capture guard using the existing device.is_capturing would let the default stay on for the uncaptured case.
Remaining gaps

Smaller things that will each cost somebody an afternoon

Medium  Two solvers in Newton combine friction differently

FeatherPGS forms the pair coefficient as the arithmetic mean of the two shape values. Newton's own MuJoCo path takes the element-wise maximum of the same two numbers. Same model, same materials, different friction cone. In this scene the finger pads use the default 1.0 and the cube is authored at 1.2, so SolverMuJoCo grips at 1.2 and FeatherPGS grips at 1.1 — small here, but the gap widens with every material mismatch, and nothing in the model tells you which rule is in force.

Medium  Friction rows are applied at two different points by default

The two friction impulses of a contact pair are equal and opposite but are applied at each body's own witness point. Those points are separated along the normal by the penetration depth, so the pair leaves a residual torque proportional to grip force times penetration — exactly the geometry of a pinch grasp. contact_friction_shared_anchor=True puts both at the midpoint and removes it. It defaults to off. Measured effect here is small (0.82 mm to 0.80 mm) only because the corrected run barely penetrates.

Medium  A documented friction parameter does nothing

friction_smoothing is accepted by the constructor, documented as the Huber-norm delta for friction velocity normalisation, and stored on the instance. No kernel reads it, and norm_huber appears nowhere in the package. It is live in the sibling Featherstone and semi-implicit solvers, which is where the parameter was copied from. Setting it is a silent no-op.

Medium  The best friction formulation is not the default

Three alternatives to the default lagged cone exist. Two of them are clearly better for this grasp: bisection cuts peak creep from 0.72 mm to 0.44 mm, and bisection with the de Saxée correction to 0.40 mm — a 44% improvement for about 1.4× the solve time, with no other change. The bracketed Newton variant is worse than the default at 0.96 mm. None of them can be selected unless pgs_mode="matrix_free", and all are excluded from the propagation contact path entirely.

Low  Model data the solver ignores

None of joint_damping, joint_friction, or joint_target_mode appears anywhere in the package — zero matches across all three files. SolverMuJoCo reads all three. So passive joint damping silently vanishes, joint dry friction vanishes, and every DOF with a non-zero gain gets both a position and a velocity term regardless of the drive mode the model declares. A model that behaves one way under SolverMuJoCo behaves differently under FeatherPGS for reasons that are invisible from the scene description. (Restitution is unread by both solvers, so that one is not a FeatherPGS gap.)

Low  Depenetration is unbounded on the path a gripper uses

The matrix-free and propagation paths clamp the Baumgarte push with rigid_body_max_depenetration_velocity. The dense path — which is where articulated contacts land under the default articulated_contact_response="immediate" — does not take that array as an argument at all. A bad initial grasp pose gets an unbounded correction.

Medium  Joint limits are silently wrong with two of the four solve kernels

The docstring states that enable_joint_limits is incompatible with pgs_kernel="tiled_contact" and "streaming". Nothing enforces it. Those kernels never receive the row-type or row-parent arrays, so limit rows are projected as if they were contacts. This is wrong physics, not a performance downgrade, and it happens without any message.

Low  Current control targets are not supported

FeatherPGS reads the deprecated DOF-layout aliases control.joint_target_pos and joint_target_vel. Under Newton's coordinate-layout targets those properties raise, so step() fails outright; under the older layout they emit a deprecation warning on every step. The scene used here has to disable the newer layout to run at all.

What to fix

In order of how much grasp stability each one buys

Add per-joint gravity compensation

The inverse-dynamics pass already computes the bias containing gravity. A per-DOF opt-in that subtracts the gravity part — the shape MuJoCo already exposes — is the single change that turns this scene from "never starts" into "0.82 mm". Nothing else on this list comes close.

Raise the default row budget and make overflow loud

Thirty-two rows is ten contacts; a two-finger grasp on a table already needs seventy. Size the default from the model's contact capacity, and warn once when a contact is dropped. Silent contact deletion is the failure mode most likely to be mistaken for a friction problem.

Make the better friction formulation reachable, and reconcile the combination rule

The de Saxée bisection cone halves the creep of the default for about 1.4× the solve time, but only exists on one solve mode and not on the propagation path. Separately, FeatherPGS averages the two friction coefficients while Newton's own MuJoCo path takes their maximum — two solvers in one library disagreeing about what a material means. Default the shared friction anchor on while you are there; the torque couple it removes is specific to pinch grasps.

Give hard contacts a way to express a squeeze

Two opposed contacts on a rigid object have no force balance to converge to, so the position-correction term becomes the only thing setting the answer — and it throws the object out in proportion to the overlap. A compliance term is the standard remedy, and dense_contact_compliance already provides one for the row family a gripper's contacts land on. The free-body row family — where an object squeezed between world-fixed pads ends up — has no compliance term at all; its diagonal is seeded with pgs_cfm and nothing else. Until that is filled in, a hard-contact grasp has to be driven, never wedged.

Keep the speculative term in the velocity pass

One hard-coded contact_speculative_scale=0.0 turns every not-yet-touching contact into a wall and makes pgs_velocity_iterations a net negative on any scene with a normal collision gap.

Move the tiled solve's staging to dynamic shared memory

A quadratic static allocation puts the row budget at the mercy of the GPU: 150 rows on Ampere, about 220 here. Until it moves, validate dense_max_constraints against the device's actual limit at construction and say so, rather than failing as a module-load error inside the first step. The solver already has an estimator that does exactly this for a different kernel.

Guard the double-buffer fork against graph capture

Use the existing device.is_capturing to fall back to an inline memset, or default double_buffer to off. Add a test that captures step() — there is currently none, which is why this has gone unnoticed.

Remove the ungated per-step debug copies and the dead parameters

Three wp.copy calls run on every step for buffers nothing reads. friction_smoothing is documented and inert. Both cost trust more than they cost time.

Port to the current control-target layout

Reading the deprecated DOF-layout aliases makes FeatherPGS unusable with Newton's current default and noisy with the old one.
